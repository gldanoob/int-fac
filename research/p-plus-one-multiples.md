# Splitting from a multiple of one hidden prime's p+1

For an odd integer $n$ with at least two distinct prime divisors, let $M>0$ be divisible by $p+1$ for **one unknown** prime $p\mid n$. A deterministic algorithm finds a proper divisor of $n$ in time polynomial in $\log n+\log M$. It works with the binary value of $M$ and uses only Dirichlet GRH for the analytic bound. Independent kernel checks of that GRH statement are available. The factor recovered can be composite. [Dirichlet GRH verification, statements 3–5](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

This gives complete factorization of every Lucas–Carmichael number from $N$ alone, of every squarefree integer from one value of $\sigma(N)$, and of every integer from one value of Dedekind's $\psi(N)$. With an ordinary divisor-sum oracle, arbitrary integers need at most $1+\lfloor\log_2\max_{p\mid N}v_p(N)\rfloor$ adaptive queries. Obtaining those values on general inputs remains an algorithmic cost.

## Two small primes and a quadratic equation

Fix the promised prime and one other prime of $n$ for the proof. Call them $u,v$ in the order $\nu_2(u+1)\ge\nu_2(v+1)$. The algorithm needs neither prime.

1. Choose a prime $\ell\equiv3\bmod4$, coprime to $n$, with $(-\ell/u)=(-\ell/v)=-1$. The residue mod 4 and the two Legendre symbols prescribe a nonempty coset of index 8 in the units modulo $4n$, by CRT. Choose an odd prime $r$ coprime to $n\ell$ with $(r/\ell)=+1$, $(r/u)=-1$, $(r/v)=+1$. These prescribe another nonempty index-8 coset, modulo $4n\ell$. Quadratic reciprocity gives $(-\ell/r)=(r/\ell)=1$. In each coset the least prime is $O(\log^2 n)$, uniformly in the unknown factors. Enumerate both parameters in growing boxes, filtering only by $\ell\bmod4$, coprimality and $(-\ell/r)=1$. Proper gcds with $n$ can be returned immediately. For a bounded decision procedure use the explicit bound with index 8 first at $4n$ and then at $4n$ times the first upper bound, taking a monotone majorant and treating the finitely many small inputs directly. [Lamzouri–Li–Soundararajan, Theorem 1.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3595#page=3)
2. Find a primitive solution to $x^2+\ell y^2=r z^2$. Legendre's criterion applies because the coefficients are squarefree, pairwise coprime, have both signs, and the required square classes modulo $\ell,r$ are $r,-\ell$ respectively. Holzer's bound gives $|x|\le\sqrt{\ell r}$, $|y|\le\sqrt r$, $0<z\le\sqrt\ell$. Scan positive $y,z$ and test $rz^2-\ell y^2$ for being a square. A nonzero solution has $x,y,z$ all nonzero, and signs may be chosen positive. At $s=u,v$, if $s\mid z$ then inertness forces $s\mid x,y$, contradicting primitivity. Thus $\gcd(n,rz^2)>1$ at the witness supplies a proper factor. Otherwise put $\alpha=x+y\sqrt{-\ell}$ and $\gamma=\alpha/\bar\alpha$ modulo $n$. Its denominator is a unit and its norm is one. [Cochrane–Mitchell, *Small solutions of the Legendre equation*](https://www.math.ksu.edu/~cochrane/research/legendre.pdf)
3. At either $s=u,v$, conjugation is Frobenius on $\mathbb F_{s^2}$. The norm-one group is cyclic of order $s+1$, and Euler's criterion gives
   $$
   \gamma^{(s+1)/2}=\alpha^{-(s^2-1)/2}
     =\left(\frac{N(\alpha)}s\right)=\left(\frac rs\right).
   $$
   Its order at $u$ therefore has 2-adic valuation exactly $\nu_2(u+1)$. At $v$ the valuation is at most $\nu_2(v+1)-1$. This strict separation includes ties in $\nu_2(s+1)$.
4. Write $M=2^j d$ with $d$ odd. Compute $\gamma^{d2^i}-1$ for $0\le i\le j$. Take the gcd of $n$ and **both** coordinates at each step. A prime divides the gcd exactly when the local power is the identity. The chain reaches the identity at the promised prime. If it never does at the other prime, the last gcd separates them. If it does at both, their odd order parts divide $d$ and their first identity indices are their distinct 2-adic valuations. The earlier gcd separates them. Other primes and repeated factors of $n$ preserve properness. Every loop costs polynomially many bit operations. Only powers of two are removed from $M$.

An alternative yields the same signs using the prime discriminant $-\ell$. Its class number $h$ is odd by genus theory. If $\mathfrak r\mid r$ in the quadratic field then $\mathfrak r^h=(\alpha)$ and $N(\alpha)=r^h$. Count reduced forms to get $h=O(\ell)$, lift a root of the integral basis polynomial to $r^h$, and take a shortest vector of the resulting ideal lattice by binary Gauss reduction. Its squared length is $r^h$, because every nonzero element has norm $r^h$ times a positive integer and a generator attains equality. The lattice index has $O(h\log r)$ bits. The GP alternative uses a primitive representation of the already factored integer $r^h$. [Genus number, Proposition 3.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.14983#page=12) · [Schoof, quadratic ideals and reduction](https://reneschoof.github.io/mcpaper.pdf)

For example, $935=5\cdot11\cdot17$, $M=936$, $\ell=23$, and $N(37+6\sqrt{-23})=13^3$. At exponents $117,234,468$ the coordinate gcds are $17,85,935$. A single run exposes all three primes in this example.

## Consequences for complete factorization

- For a **Lucas–Carmichael number** keep the original $M=N+1$ at every recursive split. Each prime $p\mid N$ satisfies $p+1\mid N+1$. Test primality at the leaves and handle prime powers by integer roots. There are fewer than $\log_2 N$ internal splits for odd inputs. This also recognizes the family on arbitrary inputs in deterministic polynomial time. Reject even integers and primes, reject a composite when the bounded search and perfect-power step yield no split, then verify squarefreeness and each divisibility against the original $N$. Wright proved this family infinite. [Wright, *There are infinitely many elliptic Carmichael numbers*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.00231)
- For **squarefree $N$**, $\sigma(N)=\prod_{p\mid N}(p+1)$, so the single original value or any positive multiple works throughout. Strip factors of 2 first. More generally, if every $v_p(N)$ is odd, each $\sigma(p^{v_p(N)})$ is divisible by $p+1$.
- For **arbitrary $N$**, Dedekind's $\psi(N)=\prod_{p^e\parallel N}p^{e-1}(p+1)$ is always a common multiple. One exact primitive four-square count on the odd part $m$ therefore suffices. In fact $r_4^{\rm prim}(m)=8\psi(m)$ for signed ordered representations including zero coordinates. From Jacobi's $r_4(m)=8\sigma(m)$, separate representations by the gcd of their coordinates and invert over square divisors. The local contribution at $p^e$ is $\sigma(p^e)-\sigma(p^{e-2})=p^{e-1}(p+1)$ for $e\ge2$, and $p+1$ for $e=1$. An odd part equal to 1 is immediate. [An arithmetic proof of Jacobi's four-square theorem](https://people.math.carleton.ca/~williams/papers/pdf/229.pdf)
- For **arbitrary $N$ with an ordinary $\sigma$ oracle**, one value $M=\sigma(m)$ recovers every prime of odd valuation in an odd $m$. At a square branch return an empty list, at a prime return that prime. At a nonsquare composite $a$, an odd-valuation prime exists. With the invariant that its odd-valuation primes occur to odd valuation in $m$, at least one $p+1$ divides the original $M$. Obtain $d\mid a$ from the splitter, taking an integer root for a prime power if needed. Put $e=a/d$, $g=\gcd(d,e)$ and recurse on $d/g,e/g$. These children are coprime, and their product is $a/g^2$, so their odd-valuation prime sets partition the parent's, including when the split overlapped. Remove all powers of each recovered prime from $m$. The quotient is a square. Take its square root and repeat with a fresh oracle answer only at a nonsquare; double the multiplicity weight after each root. The maximum remaining exponent halves each round, giving the stated query bound, at most $O(\log\log N)$. All queries are on integers at most $N$, and each inner recursion has polynomial size. Known factors conversely give $\sigma$ by its product formula. On odd inputs Jacobi's identity also allows ordinary four-square counts as the oracle. The proof gives one original $\sigma$ value for squarefree inputs and adaptive access for the general case.

## A bounded missing multiplier

Given $E,H\ge1$, define $R_s^\pm=(s\pm1)/\gcd(s\pm1,E)$. If either $R_p^+\le H$ or $R_p^-\le H$ for one unknown prime $p$ of an odd $n$ with at least two distinct prime divisors, a proper split costs $\sqrt H\,\mathrm{poly}(\log n+\log E+\log H)$ deterministic bit operations. For the plus case choose the same witness $\gamma$ as above. For the minus case use a rational unit $\gamma$ with Legendre signs $-,+$ at two prime divisors ordered by decreasing $\nu_2(s-1)$, again a fixed-index Dirichlet coset. At the covered prime the order of $\gamma^E$ divides $R_p^\pm$. In both cases the chosen base has separating 2-parts *before* exponentiation by $E$.

Write $V_k(t)=w^k+w^{-k}$ with $t=w+w^{-1}$ and $w=\gamma^E$. In the plus case first compute the trace of $\gamma$ as $2(x^2-\ell y^2)/(rz^2)$ modulo $n$. In either case $V_k$ is computable by binary doubling in $O(\log(2k))$ modular operations. Modulo any prime divisor, take $w$ in an algebraic closure. Split and inert cases both satisfy
$$
 V_j(t)=V_i(t)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w^{j-i}=1\ \text{or}\ w^{j+i}=1,
$$
by factoring the quadratic with roots $w^i,w^{-i}$. Also $V_j(t)=2$ exactly when $w^j=1$. The scalar tests suffice to distinguish primes even when $n$ has repeated factors.

To test a prefix $[1,h]$, let $s=\lfloor\sqrt h\rfloor$. For each full block of width $2s+1$ form its center $j$. The differences and sums $j-i,j+i$ for $0\le i\le s$ cover exactly the block. Evaluate the monic polynomial $\prod_{i=0}^s(X-V_i(t))$ at all centers' traces and gcd the product of the evaluations with $n$. Test the final partial block directly. Product and remainder trees over $\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$ cost $\sqrt h\,\mathrm{poly}(\log n+\log h)$ bit operations with fast polynomial multiplication, and use only monic divisions. Binary search gives the least hit $k\le H$. If none, advance the parameters. A hit gives a gcd at $\gamma^{Ek}$. If it contains some but not all of $n$, it splits $n$. If it equals $n$, both selected local orders divide $Ek$, and running the original 2-part chain at $Ek$ separates them. On a witness, a hit is guaranteed. This proof also works when the first hit is at an unrelated prime. Iterating bounded searches for the two signs costs a polynomial factor independent of $H$. Classical stage-two algorithms already search small missing factors using closely related trace progressions. [Montgomery–Kruppa, §§1, 3](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-79456-1_12) · [Źrałek, Remark 4.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0707.4102#page=6) · [Hittmeir, Lemma 3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.16729#page=4)

## Why the two public shifts do not cover all inputs

There are infinitely many balanced $N=pq$, $p<q<2p$, for which all four residuals for $p\pm1,q\pm1$ at $E=N^2-1$ are $\Theta(\sqrt N)$, each containing a prime greater than $N^{19/72}$. Here is a deduction from Ping Xi's simultaneous shifted-prime theorem. Its proof at the common cutoff $X^{19/36}$ gives $\gg X/\log X$ primes up to $X$ with a larger prime factor in each of their two shifts. Discard primes below a sufficiently small fixed multiple of $X$ and take one of the remaining boundedly many dyadic intervals containing $\gg X/\log X$ candidates. [Xi, Theorem 1.2 and §6](https://www.impan.pl/shop/en/publication/transaction/download/product/92216)

For each sign pair, bound the number of candidate pairs sharing a divisor $d>D$ of the indicated two shifts. For $D<d\le\sqrt X$, Brun–Titchmarsh in each of the two unit residue classes bounds the sum by $O((X/\log X)^2\sum_{d>D}\varphi(d)^{-2})$. For $\sqrt X<d\le X+1$, sum $(X/d+1)^2$ to get $O(X^{3/2})$. The series converges (its Euler product has local factors $1+O(1/p^2)$). A fixed sufficiently large $D$ leaves two distinct candidates for which all four cross-gcds are at most $D$. Now $\gcd(p\pm1,N^2-1)=\gcd(p\pm1,q^2-1)\le D^2$, and likewise exchanging $p,q$. The large prime in each shift survives because it exceeds $D$ and cannot divide the other prime's shifts. Its size exceeds $X^{19/36}\ge N^{19/72}$. This obstructs using a small numerical or smooth multiplier to cover the **full group orders** through $N\pm1$. Individual bases can still have smaller local orders. [Montgomery–Vaughan, *The large sieve*](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0025579300004708)
