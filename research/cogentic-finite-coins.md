# Sampling the deterministic order extractor with finitely many coins

Google's deterministic paper proves that many pairs of polynomials give a checkable order multiple for a specified prime base. Enumerating the entire four-coordinate perturbation box accounts for almost all of its worst-case time. Sampling a small box inside it gives a separate randomized algorithm with an explicit bound on the random bits for each trial. With the **original published parameters** and proved Dirichlet GRH this produces every prime factor in expected time $\exp(O((\ln N)^{0.44}))$ on each input. It also gives a polynomial-size advice list that covers all inputs of a specified bit length. [Cogentic, Lemmas 7.5, 8.2 and Proposition 10.1](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/blob/4a7bc8ab0bc770e71a259b7966c500496b99010a/cogentic_research_papers/deterministic_factoring_l34/paper.pdf) · [Dirichlet theorem verification](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

## The interval of bases

Write $L=\ln N$ and retain $B=2^{L^{.36}+O(1)}$ from the paper. After handling primes, even numbers and perfect powers, trial divide through $B$ and do the original gcd checks for $1\le a\le A$. The only use of the larger $K_{\mathrm{idx}}$ in §§8–10 is to ensure primes up to $B$ are units. The index argument of §11 is replaced by the single-base gcd test. If $p,q$ are distinct prime divisors, order them by decreasing 2-adic valuation of $p-1,q-1$. Choose the character $(\cdot/p)$ if the valuations differ and $(\cdot/p)(\cdot/q)$ when they agree. An exponent certificate for any prime base with character $-1$ then gives a proper divisor by successive squaring. The algorithm does not need the character. [Single-base proof and hypothesis audit](cogentic-dense-exponents.md)

It suffices to scan primes in $B-h<\ell\le B$ where, for instance, $h=\lceil\sqrt B\rceil(1+\lceil\log_2 N\rceil)^3$. For small inputs cap $h$ at $B/2$. Under Dirichlet GRH the von Mangoldt sums up to $x$ for the nonprincipal character and the principal character are respectively $O(\sqrt x\log^2(2Nx))$ and $x+O(\sqrt x\log^2(2Nx))$ uniformly for $x\asymp B$. This is the usual truncated explicit formula, with primes dividing $N$ contributing at most $O(\log N\log x)$. Differences at $B$ and $B-h$ and the bound $O(\sqrt B\log^2 B)$ on all higher prime powers show that the signed prime sum is $o(h)$ in logarithmic weight and the unsigned one is $(1+o(1))h$. Each character value on the surviving primes is $\pm1$. Thus $(1/2+o(1))h/\log B$ primes in this short interval have the required sign. Proposition 9.3(1) excludes at most $128\exp(200L^{.005})=o(h/\log B)$ generator primes in the **whole** dyad, so some admissible negative-sign prime lies in our interval. Its location may depend on $N$. The same conclusion holds for a retuning that proves Proposition 10.1 for every generator prime. [Cogentic, Propositions 7.1 and 9.3](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/blob/4a7bc8ab0bc770e71a259b7966c500496b99010a/cogentic_research_papers/deterministic_factoring_l34/paper.pdf#page=47) · [Davenport, multiplicative number theory](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4757-5927-3)

This reduces the number of target integers inspected deterministically from $B/2$ to $B^{1/2+o(1)}$. Trial division through $B$ and the cost of one extraction still enter the full complexity. Scanning the entire original dyad instead also gives every time bound below.

## Uniform integer boxes

The proof of Proposition 10.1 uses four independent integer coordinates with Gaussian mass proportional to $\exp(-\pi((k-\bar u_j)/S_j)^2)$, where $S_0=UA,S_1=U$ and $\bar u_j=L_0^2S_j$ are integers. Both widths are powers of two. For each of the two fields draw a coordinate uniformly in each of the consecutive blocks
$$
 [\bar u_j-2S_j,\bar u_j+2S_j)\cap\mathbb Z,\qquad j=0,1.
$$
The four samples cost exactly $4\log_2 U+2\log_2 A+8$ random bits. They lie in $\mathcal B_U^2$ for large inputs. For $S_j\ge1$, $\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\exp(-\pi(k/S_j)^2)\le 1+S_j$ by comparison with its integral. On the sampled interval, every point therefore has Gaussian probability at least $e^{-4\pi}/(2S_j)$. Consequently the probability of any event under the uniform distribution is at most $(e^{4\pi}/2)^4$ times its probability under the Gaussian. For a fixed good base, failure of the rank certificate has Gaussian probability $o(1)$ by Proposition 10.1(2), uniformly in the base and input. The uniform box thus succeeds with probability $1-o(1)$ as well. No analytic Gaussian sampling procedure is needed.

For each trial draw **one** four-coordinate vector, then use it in the deterministic search at every prime in the stated interval. Use the original congruence construction of the two polynomials for each prime, apply TryPair and verify the exponent by powering. When the repeated-squaring gcd test produces a proper divisor, return it. Fix for analysis any one admissible negative-sign prime for this input. It alone gives success probability $1-o(1)$, so no independence between bases that reuse the same coordinates is required. A single TryPair costs $\operatorname{poly}(A,B,d,P_{\rm bit},D_{\rm row},L)$; its two extra integer arguments have $O(L)$ bits. With the original parameters this is $\exp(O(L^{.44}))$. Trial division, preprocessing, scanning at most $B$ targets and testing every exponent are covered by the same bound. Repeat with fresh uniform bits, dovetailing ordinary trial division to guarantee termination on every tape, including bounded inputs outside the asymptotic guards. Verified prime recognition and recursion through at most $2\log_2 N$ nodes give the claimed expected complete-factorization bound. This route uses the deterministic manuscript's smooth relation supply. It does not use the recursive randomized descent of the other manuscript. [Cogentic, Lemma 7.5 and §11.2](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/blob/4a7bc8ab0bc770e71a259b7966c500496b99010a/cogentic_research_papers/deterministic_factoring_l34/paper.pdf#page=34)

Under the [two-thirds parameter transfer](cogentic-deterministic-two-thirds.md), $\log U=O(L^{2/3}(\log L)^{1/3})$ while $\log A,\log B=O(L^{1/3}(\log L)^{2/3})$. Exactly the same reduction then gives per-input expected complete factorization in $L_N[1/3,O(1)]$ with at most $O(L^{2/3}(\log L)^{1/3})$ random bits in each splitting trial. This corollary depends on the new uniform analytic estimates, beyond the published theorem.

## Advice and its cost

For each bit length $b$, pad the trial seed to the maximum required length at any $b$-bit input, ignoring unused suffixes. For all sufficiently large $b$ the failure probability is below $1/2$ for each eligible composite. Among $b+1$ independent seeds the probability that all fail on any one of the fewer than $2^b$ inputs is below $1/2$ by a union bound. Consequently a single list of $b+1$ seeds works simultaneously for all composites of that length requiring the extractor. Lists for lengths at most $b$ handle recursion, with polynomial total advice. The finite exceptional lengths can be handled by trial division. Locating such lists uniformly and within the same time bound remains open. ## A limit of changing the search parameters alone

Let $s=\log U$, $a=\log A$, $h=\log B$, and suppose an exhaustive perturbation search keeps the following four scale inequalities, with fixed implicit constants:
$$
 s\gg L/d,\qquad s\gg da,\qquad a\gg h,\qquad
 a\gg u\log(2u),\quad u\gg s/h .
$$
The first is the modulus-versus-width requirement in the individual progression bound, the second ensures the perturbed coefficients dominate their rounding errors, the third accommodates the progression sieve, and the fourth pays the reciprocal smooth density in the diagonal moment bound. These are sufficient requirements used by the current proof. They imply
$$
                   s\gg L^{2/3}(\log L)^{1/3}.
$$
Indeed if $s\ge L^{.7}$ the conclusion follows directly. Otherwise $d\gg L^{.3}$ and $h\ll s/d$, so $u\gg d$ and $\log(2u)\gg\log L$. The smooth-density requirement and $a\gg h$ give $a^2\gg s\log L$. Combined with $a\ll s/d$ this forces $s\gg d^2\log L$. Substitution of $d\gg L/s$ proves the claim. Enumerating even one whole coordinate of length $U$ must therefore pay this scale under these inequalities. Improving the exponent by this route requires a different search or a weaker inequality. This observation is about the parameter constraints in this proof only.
