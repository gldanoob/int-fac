# Coverage of two small tower polynomials at unrestricted modular roots

For $X\le N\le2X$, set $L=\log X$, $D=(L/\log L)^{1/3}$, $W=D\log L$, and $V=DW$. Consider primitive irreducible integer polynomials of degree $O(D)$ and Mahler measure at most $\exp(O(V))$ whose root field admits a tower of quadratic extensions from $\mathbb Q$. There are $X^{o(1)}$ such polynomials up to a choice of sign, by the [tower-polynomial count](https://github.com/gldanoob/int-fac/blob/66dd6ff/research/cogentic-full-tower-height-scarcity.md). Every counting conclusion below also holds for fields with a tower of possibly non-Galois steps whose maximum relative degree is uniformly $o(D)$, since the same count holds for that larger class. For bounded degree this also follows by counting coefficient vectors directly. The relevant identities are $DV=L$ and $D^2W=L$.

Two different such polynomials $P,Q$ can have a common root modulo $N$ on at most $X^{o(1)}$ inputs in the whole interval, even if the root can be any residue and both polynomials are chosen separately for each input. The same conclusion holds for roots $u,v$ at which a nonzero bivariate polynomial $G(u,v)$ vanishes modulo $N$, subject to the following conditions. Its bidegree $(s,t)$ is bounded, its coefficient sum satisfies $\log\max(2,\|G\|_1)=O(W)$, and $G$ does not vanish on any pair of complex roots of $P,Q$. The polynomial $G$ may also be chosen afresh for every $N$.

## Elimination before counting inputs

Let $d=\deg P$, $e=\deg Q$, and form the successive integer resultants
$$R(X)=\operatorname{Res}_Y(Q(Y),G(X,Y)),\qquad T=\operatorname{Res}_X(P(X),R(X)).$$
The Bézout identity for the first resultant gives $R(u)=0\pmod N$. The second gives $N\mid T$. These are polynomial identities over the integers and hold even at primes dividing the leading coefficients or at repeated prime powers. The assumption on complex roots gives $T\ne0$. Since $\deg R\le es$, the product formulas give
$$ |T|\le M(P)^{es}M(Q)^{dt}\,\|G\|_1^{de},$$
where $M$ denotes Mahler measure. Indeed, each $G(\alpha,\beta)$ is bounded by $\|G\|_1\max(1,|\alpha|)^s\max(1,|\beta|)^t$. The leading coefficient powers in the two resultants are covered by the corresponding Mahler factors. Thus $\log\max(2,|T|)=O(L)$. There are $X^{o(1)}$ choices of the two polynomials and $\exp(O(W))$ choices of $G$. Each nonzero $T$ has at most $\exp(O(L/\log L))$ positive divisors. This proves the count with all choices charged to the input.

For a common root take $G=X-Y$. The zero-resultant exception then means $P$ and $Q$ agree up to sign. More generally a fractional linear relation with short integer coefficients is covered by the bidegree-$(1,1)$ case after clearing its denominator. If $s<d$, $t<e$ and $G(\alpha,\beta)=0$ in the algebraic closure of the rationals, neither specialized polynomial in the other argument is identically zero, by minimality of $P,Q$. Consequently $[\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta):\mathbb Q(\alpha)]\le t$ and $[\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta):\mathbb Q(\beta)]\le s$. When $s=t=1$ there is an actual equality of the two embedded fields. This identifies the algebraic exceptions a construction could deliberately use.

If the three modular equalities hold at only one prime $p\mid N$ with $p\ge X^\eta$ for fixed $0<\eta<1$, then $p\mid T$. There are at most $O(L)$ possible prime divisors for each nonzero $T$. Their union over all choices has size $X^{o(1)}$. Each eligible prime divides $O(X^{1-\eta})$ integers in the interval. Thus coverage via this weaker local condition is at most $X^{1-\eta+o(1)}$. This argument concerns the two specified low-height tower presentations and a short alignment. The polynomial square-kernel procedure still applies to arbitrary explicit marks, large heights, and deliberately overlapping fields.

## Longer alignments and inputs with few prime factors

For all inputs, the same proof works if $s,t$ and $\log\max(2,\|G\|_1)/W$ are all $o(\log L)$. There are still only $X^{o(1)}$ possible coefficient arrays, and the determinant has $o(L\log L)$ bits. The divisor bound remains $X^{o(1)}$ uniformly in this range.

For semiprimes there is a stronger extension. Allow $G$ to be described by a division-free arithmetic program in two indeterminates, using integer constants and $+,-,\times$, with total binary description length $b=o(L)$ including all constants and references. The degree is at most $2^{O(b)}$, and $\log\max(2,\|G\|_1)\le 2^{O(b)}$ by induction over the gates using submultiplicativity of the coefficient sum. Hence the resultant bound gives $\log\max(2,|T|)=X^{o(1)}$. An integer with this bit length has at most $X^{o(1)}$ different prime divisors. It has at most $X^{o(1)}$ semiprime divisors, since each such divisor is specified by two primes from the list, with repetition allowed. Summing over the $2^{o(L)}$ programs and both tower presentations preserves the bound. This applies also to inputs with any fixed bound on the number of prime factors counted with multiplicity. No balanced-factor promise is needed. The condition that $G$ vanish on no pair of characteristic-zero roots remains essential.

In particular a comparison between products of a total of $k$ explicitly chosen linear marks with $O(W)$-bit coefficients on the two sides, allowing a difference of products after clearing denominators, has a description of $O(k(W+\log(2+k)))$ bits. For $k=o(L/W)$ the preceding semiprime bound applies. It concerns alignments expressed on the modular roots themselves. An uncertified ratio of algebraic square roots does not give such an alignment.

## When different factors of a product carry different primes of the input

At a rational radix of numerator and denominator bounded by $\exp(O(V))$, the union of the prime divisors of *all* nonzero homogenized values from the counted polynomials has size $X^{o(1)}$. Indeed, there are only $X^{o(1)}$ polynomial/radix choices, and each evaluated integer has $O(L)$ bits. Similarly, the union of primes dividing any of the nonzero resultants for the short programs above has size $X^{o(1)}$, since each resultant has $X^{o(1)}$ bits.

Consequently even a product of arbitrarily many such nonzero values, chosen for each input with different radices or alignments, can be divisible by at most $X^{o(1)}$ distinct semiprimes in the dyadic interval when one takes the union over all products. Both primes of the semiprime must appear in the same common list of $X^{o(1)}$ primes, though they can divide different terms. This also holds with any fixed bound on the number of prime factors counted with multiplicity. If only a single prime $p\ge X^\eta$ of an arbitrary input divides some such product, the input count is at most $X^{1-\eta+o(1)}$. These conclusions permit reducible products with all terms in the counted catalogue. Unrestricted irreducible factors, unrestricted modular radices for single polynomials, and identically zero evaluations are outside that argument.

## Detecting the exact algebraic component from supplied roots

There is also a factor test in the zero-resultant case, provided the two modular roots are available. Integralize the root variables as $A=a_P X$, $B=a_Q Y$ for the respective leading coefficients. Their polynomials $\widehat P(A)=a_P^{d-1}P(A/a_P)$ and $\widehat Q(B)=a_Q^{e-1}Q(B/a_Q)$ are monic. The rational tensor product of the two fields is a product of number fields. A primitive element $Z=A+hB$ for this product has monic defining polynomial
$$\Phi_h(Z)=\prod_{\alpha,\beta}(Z-a_P\alpha-h a_Q\beta).$$
Among the $de$ ordered pairs, each two distinct pairs collide for at most one integer $h$. Once $\Phi_h$ is squarefree, its irreducible factors over $\mathbb Q$ correspond precisely to the simple factors of the tensor product.

For the supplied modular roots $u,v$, put $w=a_Pu+h a_Qv$. Then $\Phi_h(w)=0\pmod N$, by the monic resultant identity. If $\gcd(N,\operatorname{Disc}\Phi_h)=1$, every prime power dividing $N$ divides exactly one evaluated irreducible factor of $\Phi_h$. Computing its gcd with $N$ for each factor therefore recovers the partition by algebraic component. Whenever the primes select different components, the test splits the input. If all select the same component the computed partition has a single block. This test applies to unrelated presentations and can detect a split even when both $u-v$ and $u+v$ are units.

For good reduction of $\widehat P,\widehat Q$ at every prime of $N$, one can obtain a suitable $h$ or a divisor with polynomial overhead in the explicit polynomial sizes and $\log N$. Try the first $1+\binom{de}{2}$ positive integers. At any hidden prime larger than this number, the root grids are separable and each pair collision forbids at most one of these residues, so some $h$ works at that prime. Its discriminant gcd is then either one or a proper divisor. If all prime divisors are smaller, trial division through this bound suffices. When good reduction fails, a proper gcd with the initial discriminants already gives a split. A fully bad modulus requires separate handling. This procedure uses the two supplied root maps. Obtaining those maps on an arbitrary single input is a separate cost.

## Small checks

With $P=2X^2+X+25$, $Q=3Y^2+Y+5$ the residues $2,3$ are roots modulo 35. Using $G=Y-X-1$ gives $T=5075$. Using $G=XY+2X-10$ gives $T=767375$. Both are multiples of 35. Modulo 25 the roots $2,3$ of $5X^2+X+3$, $2Y^2+Y+4$ and the first alignment give $T=1025$, despite 5 dividing the first leading coefficient. The overlapping tower $P=X^4-2$, $Q=Y^2-2$, $G=X^2-Y$ gives $T=0$ exactly.

The [component splitter](../experiments/cogentic-tensor-component-split.py) checks supplied roots of monic and nonmonic polynomials. For $N=1161359$, $P=Q=X^4-10X^2+1$, $u=1072857$ and $v=119483$ both $u-v$ and $u+v$ are units. At slope $h=2$ the tensor discriminant is coprime to $N$. The factor $Z^4-58Z^2+625$ evaluated at $u+2v$ gives gcd $1009$. The replay also recovers $1009$ from two nonmonic presentations and distinguishes same-component and bad-reduction examples. Its default search tries 16 slopes and reports an inconclusive result when that budget is exhausted.
