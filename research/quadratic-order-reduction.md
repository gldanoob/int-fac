# Factoring from quadratic-order class numbers

Schoof’s Algorithm 6.4 reduces an arbitrary factoring instance to quadratic orders. A single positive polynomial-bit multiple of the order class number per composite cofactor suffices. The reduction below uses only Dirichlet GRH for its short list of split primes. It applies to repeated prime factors, where counting all reduced forms can give the wrong exponent. [Schoof, Algorithm 6.4](https://reneschoof.github.io/mcpaper.pdf) · [Independent verification of Dirichlet GRH](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

## Small split primes for every negative order discriminant

Write $D=f^2D_K<0$ and $G=\operatorname{Pic}(\mathcal O_D)$. The split prime forms of odd norm $p\le C(1+\log |D|)^2$, $p\nmid D$, generate $G/G^2$. Their projections generate the Sylow $2$-subgroup $P$. This extends the [fundamental-discriminant argument](class-group-walks.md#small-split-primes-already-cover-the-two-primary-part) to unknown conductors.

For each nontrivial character of $G/G^2$, Ibukiyama’s Proposition 3.4 supplies a reciprocal pair of fundamental divisors $\delta_1,\delta_2$. Neither is 1, since $\{1,D_K\}$ represents the trivial genus character. The pair may share a prime. On units modulo $2|D|$, their quadratic Dirichlet characters multiply to $\chi_{D_K}$. Both are nonprincipal and their product is nonprincipal, since reduction onto the units of each conductor is surjective. Thus the joint sign map has image of size four. Theorem 1.4 of Lamzouri–Li–Soundararajan supplies a prime in its $(-1,-1)$ coset within the claimed bound, with small moduli absorbed in the constant. This prime avoids $2D$, splits in $K$, and its prime form has genus character $-1$ by Ibukiyama’s Theorem 3.5. The primes in the list therefore detect every nontrivial character of $G/G^2$. Every maximal subgroup of the finite abelian $2$-group $P$ contains $P^2$, so generation modulo squares gives generation of $P$ after projection. Enumeration checks the Kronecker symbol and constructs a form above each small split prime directly from $D$. The unknown conductor and the reciprocal pair occur only in the proof. [Ibukiyama, Proposition 3.4 and Theorem 3.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.14983#page=12) · [Lamzouri–Li–Soundararajan, Theorem 1.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3595#page=3)

Upstream gives a polynomial-time list generating the **full** class group with prime cutoff $O((1+\log |D|)^{32})$, using a Hecke zero-free strip and effective estimates. Its PR 109 turns a sufficiently deep root of a prescribed odd-order element into the exact two-primary projection. The shorter list above supplies all projections required for that factoring step. Constructing the roots or comparable annihilators remains the expensive part. [Pinned upstream generator theorem](https://github.com/1337isnot1337/integer-factoring/blob/1b331929bb774ae8dcb8f50cdd10a3243b7c649f/research/polylog-prime-form-generators.md) · [Pinned upstream root identity](https://github.com/1337isnot1337/integer-factoring/blob/1b331929bb774ae8dcb8f50cdd10a3243b7c649f/research/deep-class-root-projection.md)

## One class-number query per composite cofactor

Strip factors 2 and 3, test primality, and replace perfect powers by their bases, retaining multiplicities. For a remaining composite $n$, set $D=-n$ if $n\equiv3\bmod4$, and $D=-3n$ otherwise. Request any positive multiple $M$ of $h(D)=|G|$ with polynomially many bits. For each listed form $g$, raising it to the odd part of $M$ kills its odd component and applies an automorphism to its two-primary component. The resulting elements generate $P$. Even a different positive multiple of the order of each listed $g$ suffices, since each resulting power generates the same projected cyclic subgroup. [Schoof, Algorithm 6.4, pp. 270–272](https://reneschoof.github.io/mcpaper.pdf)

Use Schoof’s ambiguous-form extraction. In the $D=-n$ branch, a nonidentity involution gives a proper split. In the $D=-3n$ branch start with the known class of $(3,3,(n+3)/4)$ and adjoin the projected generators. As soon as the subgroup is noncyclic, extract an involution outside the known order-two subgroup and decode a proper split. Genus theory guarantees this occurs for the remaining composite inputs. Schoof gives polynomial cost for this step, including calculations inside the cyclic two-group accumulated before the split. Testing only the final involution of each individual generator can miss a second independent involution. For instance, $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$ generate $C_4\times C_2$ and both double to $(2,0)$. [Schoof, correctness and running time of Step 3](https://reneschoof.github.io/mcpaper.pdf)

The input to each query has at most $b+2$ bits. A binary tree of proper splits has at most $b$ leaves on a $b$-bit input, counting prime factors with multiplicity. Perfect-power replacement can only reduce the work. Thus there are $O(b)$ class-number queries and polynomial total work beyond them. For a nondecreasing query bound $Q$, the cost is $O(bQ(b+2))+\operatorname{poly}(b)$. Complete factorization gives the requested smallest prime factor.

Two squareful examples exercise the two branches. For $n=175$, cubing the norm-23 prime form at $D=-175$ gives $(7,7,8)$ and the divisor 7. For $n=325$, the fourth power of the norm-11 form at $D=-975$ is $(16,7,16)$. Here $(32-7)(32+7)=25\cdot39=975$ and $\gcd(39,325)=13$. [Reproducible PARI checks](../experiments/primitive-forms.gp)

## Counting primitive reduced forms without the conductor

Tulsyan’s two counts handle the signs and ties of reduced forms. For nonfundamental $D$ they include imprimitive forms. At $D=-99$ the representatives are $(1,1,25)$, $(5,1,5)$ and $(3,3,9)$. The first two are primitive, so the raw count 3 fails even as a multiple of the class number 2. Remark 6.1 already obtains the same all-order asymptotic bound by factoring the discriminant, listing its square divisors and applying Möbius inversion. The direct description below avoids paying for that preliminary factorization, which matters when transferring any future speedup to factoring. [Tulsyan, §§5.1 and 6](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00115v2#page=5)

Here is a fixed-size addition to both descriptions. Put $m=|b'|$ for a reduced form $(a,b',c)$, so $a,c>0$ and $m$ may be zero. Introduce seven natural variables $d,x,y,u,v,w,t$ and require

$$
a=dx,\qquad m=dy,\qquad yu+1=xv,\qquad dw+1=ct,\qquad u<x,\quad w<c.
$$

The first inverse equation forces $\gcd(x,y)=1$, hence $d=\gcd(a,m)$. The second forces $\gcd(d,c)=1$. Conversely a primitive triple determines $d,x,y$. It determines $u$ as the unique residue in $[0,x)$ with $yu\equiv-1\bmod x$, and $w$ as the unique residue in $[0,c)$ with $dw\equiv-1\bmod c$. The quotients $v,t$ are then forced. For modulus 1 its unique residue is zero. This also covers $m=0$ and $c=1$. Every primitive form contributes exactly once and every imprimitive form contributes zero.

All witnesses fit in the original box. Reduction gives $a,m,c\le |D|$. The bounds $v\le\max(1,y)$ and $t\le\max(1,d)$ follow from $u<x$ and $w<c$. The equations use six extra products, each in one chain, small nonnegative coefficients, two comparisons and a constant number of variables. Tulsyan’s Theorem 3.1, Proposition 3.2 and Theorem 4.1 then give, on the preprint’s counting-engine assumptions, a deterministic $2^{O(b/\sqrt{\log b})}$ algorithm for the primitive class number of **every** negative quadratic order from its discriminant alone. This is a paper instantiation of the generic theorem. Theorem 1.2(ii) in the preprint itself is restricted to fundamental discriminants. Computing these counts at the benchmark rate yields that same rate for factoring. A faster evaluator specialized to these two counts would transfer directly. [Tulsyan, §§3–4 and Theorem 1.2](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00115v2#page=4)

Lean checks existence, soundness and uniqueness of the seven arithmetic witnesses, including zero middle coefficient. The runtime transfer uses the published counting theorem on paper. [Lean proof](../formal/PrimitiveCertificate.lean)

The [finite check](../experiments/primitive-forms.gp) enumerates all witnesses on reduced forms at the 200 admissible discriminants $-400\le D\le-3$. It compares the witness count for each form with the gcd test and each total with PARI’s primitive class number.

## How much partial information buys a faster search

Suppose a certified window $[A,B]\subset\mathbb Z_{>0}$ contains $h$, with width $W=B-A$, and a certified residue $h\bmod s$ is known. From the first admissible $x_0$, solve $(g^s)^j=g^{-x_0}$ over the at most $1+\lfloor W/s\rfloor$ candidate indices by baby-step/giant-step. The hit $x=x_0+sj$ annihilates the prescribed seed, which suffices here. The search takes $O(\log B+\log s+\sqrt{1+W/s})$ group operations. Its hits are spaced by $\operatorname{lcm}(s,\operatorname{ord}(g))$ in the exponent. A unique hit determines $h$ because $h$ is one of the candidates. [Kedlaya–Sutherland, §4.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0801.2778#page=7) · [Schoof, §3](https://reneschoof.github.io/mcpaper.pdf)

With $d=|D|$, the [existing Euler window](round-3.md#class-number-windows-from-proved-dirichlet-grh) at cutoff $Q=d^\alpha$ costs $d^{\alpha+o(1)}$ and has width $d^{(1-\alpha)/2+o(1)}$. A cheaply supplied $s=d^\beta$ for fixed $0\le\beta<1/2$ changes the optimum to $\alpha=(1-2\beta)/5$. For example $\beta=1/4$ brings the total to $d^{1/10+o(1)}$. For subpower budgets use the explicit logarithmic error bounds. At target cost $T$, choosing $Q$ of order $T$ requires a certified residue modulus roughly $\sqrt d/T^{5/2}$ up to logarithmic factors, using $W\ll\sqrt{d/Q}\operatorname{polylog}d$. Genus parity alone supplies far less precision on semiprimes. Sutherland’s primorial bounded-order algorithm takes an upper bound on the element order, which may greatly exceed this window’s width. [Schoof, Theorem 6.3](https://reneschoof.github.io/mcpaper.pdf) · [Sutherland, Chapter 4](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/sutherland-phd.pdf)

## Published bounds on finding relations and orders

- Given a polynomial-size list of seeds, the diagonal rows $h(D)e_i$ are full-rank relations with $O(b)$-bit entries once the class number is available. A proposed row can be verified by binary powering and form reduction. Bisson’s Corollary 6.2 bounds the size of generating relations on his prime base under the relevant GRH, while Algorithm 6.3 samples exponent vectors randomly. Biasse–Erukulangara give an improved expected-time analysis of the Hafner–McCurley method. These results leave deterministic construction below the benchmark open. [Bisson, §6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1101.4323#page=7) · [Biasse–Erukulangara](https://doi.org/10.3934/amc.2021055)
- For an explicitly given unit modulo $N$, Harvey–Hittmeir’s Lemma 2.1 computes its order $r$ with a sufficiently large cutoff in about $\sqrt{r/\log\log r}$ times polynomial bit overhead. Sutherland’s Algorithm 4.2 with $B=N^{1/u}$, fixed $u>2$, costs $O(B)$ group operations for orders whose prime-power factors at primes exceeding $B$ have product at most $B^2$. This is an input-specific promise for the [prescribed small-base reduction](order-and-crt-reductions.md#a-linear-number-of-queries-on-one-cofactor). [Harvey–Hittmeir, Lemma 2.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11131v2#page=5) · [Sutherland, Proposition 4.7](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/sutherland-phd.pdf)
- In Hhan’s generic model with a hidden uniformly random $n$-bit prime order, the classical $\Omega(2^{n/3})$ constant-success lower bound extends to asking for any positive polynomial-bit multiple. On the formal collision-free transcript a fixed $L$-bit output has at most $L/(n-1)$ candidate prime divisors. It adds at most $O(L/2^n)$ to success beyond the collision probability in the proof. This deduction prices purely generic operations. Integer residues and quadratic forms expose additional arithmetic. [Hhan, Theorem 4.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11269#page=13)

## Sequential arbitrary halves and the deep-root oracle

Upstream PR 109 asks for any $z$ with $2^{2L}z=2^Lg$ in additive group notation. A single half of a promised square has a weaker guarantee. If $x\in2^{r+1}G$, choose a half $y_0\in2^rG$. All halves are $y_0+G[2]$, so every possible answer permits $r$ further halvings precisely when $G[2]\subseteq2^rG$. In a nontrivial Sylow subgroup with cyclic factors of orders $2^{e_i}$, an odd-order input is guaranteed $\min_i e_i$ successive calls against arbitrary answers. A shortest factor can acquire an involution at the first call and reach a nonsquare generator at the last. For the guarantee against a fixed oracle one can start at zero and prescribe these successive halves along that cyclic factor. An oracle returning the **odd-order half** of any odd-order input, on the other hand, lets $L$ halvings of $2^Lg$ recover the odd component of $g$. [Upstream PR 109, §§1–3](https://github.com/1337isnot1337/integer-factoring/blob/1b331929bb774ae8dcb8f50cdd10a3243b7c649f/research/deep-class-root-projection.md)

At $D=-4796=-4\cdot1199$ the norm-13 form $g=(13,12,95)$ has order 38 and $2^{13}g=(5,2,240)$. A fixed oracle can map $2^jg$ to $2^{j-1}g$ for $1\le j\le13$, because the inputs are distinct ($2$ has order 18 modulo 19). The chain returns $g$ on step 13, a nonsquare. Each output agreed with a half the caller already knew, while the missing involution $19g=(11,0,109)$ contains the split.

There is a generic obstruction even with adaptive promised queries. In an opaque cyclic group $C_{2p}$ with supplied generator $g$ and size bound $2^L$, simulate each known handle by an integer coefficient of $g$. Answer addition and equality on integers, and half an even coefficient by integer division. An odd coefficient is never a promised square. A deterministic polynomial-work algorithm encounters only polynomially many coefficients of polynomial bit length. Choose an $(L-1)$-bit prime $p$ avoiding the prime divisors of all nonzero differences of those coefficients, including differences from zero. The prime number theorem leaves such a $p$ for large $L$. Every equality and half answer extends consistently to that group and a fixed oracle. No output handle represents $pg$, the required nonidentity involution, since its nonzero coefficient would be divisible by $p$. This argument concerns algorithms operating on opaque group handles. Quadratic form coefficients carry additional arithmetic information.
