# Dense primes certify a shorter exponent for the full unit group

Let \(L=\log N\), \(b=\lfloor\log_2N\rfloor+1\), and \(\lambda(N)=\exp((\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times)\), where the exponent of a finite group is the least common multiple of its element orders. Cogentic's §11.2 finds a proper divisor or constructs a multiple of every \(p-1\) for \(p\mid N\). With its published parameters, the following modification constructs a proper divisor or a **multiple of \(\lambda(N)\)** with bit length at most \(\exp((\log 2+o(1))L^{.36})\). This includes the prime-power contributions to the unit group. The deterministic search for smooth relations retains its published worst-case time bound. The argument uses Dirichlet GRH over \(\mathbb Q\), proved by Alpöge and computationally verified as recorded by Sutherland. [Cogentic, §§7.5, 9.3, 10.1 and 11.2](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/blob/4a7bc8ab0bc770e71a259b7966c500496b99010a/cogentic_research_papers/deterministic_factoring_l34/paper.pdf) · [Alpöge](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) · [Sutherland](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

## Majority generation on a dyadic interval

Fix \(0<\delta<1/2\). There is an effective constant \(C_\delta\) such that, for \(N\ge 2\) and \(X\ge C_\delta\log^2(2N)\), every set of primes coprime to \(N\) containing at least \((1/2+\delta)T\) of the \(T=\pi(X)-\pi(X/2)\) primes in \((X/2,X]\) generates \((\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times\). The proportion is relative to **all** primes in the interval. The constant \(1/2\) is sharp in the limiting density, since primes congruent to \(1\) modulo \(3\) have density \(1/2\) and generate the trivial subgroup modulo \(3\).

For the proof take a nonprincipal Dirichlet character \(\chi\bmod N\) induced by a primitive character \(\chi^*\bmod q\). Fix a smooth function \(0\le w\le1\) supported inside \((1/2,1)\), equal to one on \([1/2+\eta,1-\eta]\), with \(\eta>0\) to be chosen. Its Mellin transform \(\widehat w(s)\) is \(O_w((1+|\Im s|)^{-2})\) uniformly for \(-1/2\le\Re s\le 2\), by integration by parts. Mellin inversion of \(-L'/L\), moving to \(\Re s=-1/2\), gives
\[
 \sum_n\Lambda(n)\chi^*(n)w(n/X)
 =-\sum_\rho X^\rho\widehat w(\rho)+O_w(1+X^{-1/2}\log(2q)).
\]
The optional trivial zero at zero contributes \(O_w(1)\). On the new vertical line the functional equation and absolute convergence at \(\Re(1-s)=3/2\) bound the logarithmic derivative by \(O(\log(q(|\Im s|+2)))\). The usual horizontal contours avoiding zeros justify the shift, since \(\widehat w\) decreases faster than any power. The unit-height zero count \(O(\log(q(|t|+2)))\) and \(\Re\rho=1/2\) give \(O_w(\sqrt X\log(2q))\) for the sum. Passing to \(\chi\bmod N\) costs at most \(\sum_{p\mid N}\log p\le\log N\), since at most one positive power of each prime lies in this dyadic interval. [Koukoulopoulos, *The Distribution of Prime Numbers*, Chapters 10–12](https://dms.umontreal.ca/~koukoulo/documents/publications/primes.pdf) · [Alpöge's Dirichlet theorem](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf)

Prime powers of exponent at least two contribute \(O(\sqrt X\log^2 X)=o(X)\) in absolute value. The ordinary prime number theorem bounds the two discarded edge intervals by \((2\eta+o_\eta(1))X\) in von Mangoldt weight. Since \(\log p=\log X+O(1)\) on the dyadic interval, this gives
\[
 \left|\sum_{X/2<p\le X}\chi(p)\right|\log X
 \le C_w\sqrt X\log(2N)+(2\eta+o_\eta(1))X.
\]
Choose \(2\eta<\delta/4\) and then \(C_\delta\) sufficiently large. Since \(T\log X\sim X/2\), this makes the displayed character sum strictly smaller than \(2\delta T\) in absolute value. All constants are effective. If the specified primes generated a proper subgroup, a nontrivial character of the finite abelian quotient would take value one on every specified prime. Its real sum over all interval primes would be at least \(2(1/2+\delta)T-T=2\delta T\), a contradiction.

## Construction, guards and bit cost

Keep the paper's Step 1 with its trial cutoff \(K_{\rm idx}\), its perfect-power precheck and its gcd checks on \(a-m\). This directly supplies the hypotheses under which §§8–10 establish their successful-prime density. Starting at \(M=2\), sweep the paper's entire interval \(B/2<\ell\le B\), \(B=2^{L^{.36}+O(1)}\). If \(\ell^M=1\pmod N\), continue. Otherwise run the prescribed search for this \(\ell\). Upon receiving a verified \(\ell^{E_\ell}=1\pmod N\), replace \(M\) by \(\operatorname{lcm}(M,E_\ell)\). A failed search leaves the current \(M\) available for the following prime.

For every prime in the paper's good set, Proposition 10.1(2), Lemma 7.4(3) and Lemma 7.5 guarantee either an exponent is found or the current exponent already kills that prime. Divisibility of successive candidates ensures the final \(M\) kills all these primes. Proposition 9.3(1) supplies a \(1-o(1)\) fraction of the dyadic primes for every sufficiently large surviving input. The preceding theorem at, for example, \(\delta=1/4\) now gives \(\lambda(N)\mid M\). This application is directly modulo \(N\), including for inputs with repeated factors.

Each merge has \(\operatorname{ord}_N(\ell)\mid\lambda(N)\) and \(\operatorname{ord}_N(\ell)\nmid M\) before the update. Its order divides the new \(M\). Thus \(\gcd(M,\lambda(N))\) increases at each merge by an integral factor of at least two. There are at most \(\log_2\lambda(N)\le b\) merges. Lemma 7.5 gives \(\log_2 E_\ell\le P=64d^3(2dB+1)L_0\). Consequently \(\log_2 M\le 1+bP=\exp((\log 2+o(1))L^{.36})\). Evaluating one additional modular power per interval prime and computing these least common multiples cost \(\exp(O(L^{.36}))\) bit operations overall, below the smooth-relation search cost. The same argument applies to another parameter schedule whenever it proves the stated density and gives an explicit per-exponent bit bound \(P\).

A uniform final guard can certify the answer without knowing the threshold in the density argument. For each integer \(2\le a\le\min(N-1,3b^2)\), check the gcd with \(N\), returning any proper divisor, and check \(a^M=1\pmod N\) on units. Return \(M\) if all unit tests pass. Otherwise use the paper's fallback trial division. Every proper subgroup of the unit group omits a coprime prime of size at most \(3(\log N)^2\) by Bach's theorem and Dirichlet GRH. If that bound exceeds \(N-1\), testing all units modulo \(N\) suffices. The kernel of the power map therefore equals the entire unit group whenever the guard returns an exponent. The fallback can occur only below an effective absolute threshold. This guard costs \(\operatorname{poly}(b)\log_2(2M)\) bit operations by binary powering. [Bach, *Explicit bounds for primality testing and related problems*](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1990-1023756-8) · [Lamzouri, Li and Soundararajan, introduction and Theorem 1.1](https://www.math.ksu.edu/about/personal/xiannan/papers/QuaResL1ver2Sound.pdf)

The omission from §11.2 is the **final least common multiple of the integers up to \(K_{\rm idx}\)**. Preliminary trial division and checks for factors during construction serve a separate purpose. Keeping the original preliminary cutoff gives the result above directly. One can also lower that preliminary cutoff to \(B\) by checking the hypotheses in §§8–10. Lemma 8.1 requires \(\gcd(N,P_0\ell)=1\). Lemma 8.2(1) and the factor-base construction require \(q\nmid N\) for \(q\in J_*\subseteq[1,B]\). Both follow from \(P^-(N)>B\), since \(z_0<B\) and \(\ell\le B\). The gcd checks on \(a-m\) still supply exactly the inverses used in Lemmas 7.4, 7.5 and 9.5. The only subgroup-size use of \(P^-(N)>K_{\rm idx}\) is in the replaced Lemma 11.1. This lowers the trial cutoff while preserving the costs of the remaining stages.

The finite replay checks the complete unit group independently for every \(3\le N\le384\). It uses 255 primes in \((2048,4096]\) and three selections, including a selection that preferentially removes primes surviving exponent \(\lambda(N)/2\) (that is, primes whose half-exponent power differs from one). All 1,146 selected sets generate the complete group. Across 2,292 runs using exact or inflated element-order multiples, each merge doubles the predicted gcd and no run uses more than four merges. The small-unit guard rejects all 843 tested deficient exponents. In 79 moduli \(\lambda(N)\) exceeds \(\operatorname{lcm}_{p\mid N}(p-1)\). These tests exercise the new postprocessing using directly computed finite orders. [Executable replay](../experiments/cogentic-dense-exponents.py)

## A single successful prime can give the divisor directly

For a proper divisor alone, run the following test at each verified pair \((\ell,E_\ell)\). Write \(E_\ell=2^s h\) for odd \(h\), compute \(x=\ell^h\bmod N\), and successively take \(\gcd(x-1,N)\) and replace \(x\) by \(x^2\bmod N\) until \(x=1\). Return any proper divisor encountered. Handle even numbers and perfect powers as in §11.3. The search can fall back to trial division if every target is exhausted. For sufficiently large surviving inputs the smooth-relation search and these gcd checks give a divisor before the fallback, with no call to the common-exponent assembly or to Part I of the paper. [Cogentic, Lemma 3.1(2) and §§7–11](https://github.com/google-research/google-research/blob/4a7bc8ab0bc770e71a259b7966c500496b99010a/cogentic_research_papers/deterministic_factoring_l34/paper.pdf)

Indeed, two distinct odd primes \(p,q\mid N\) exist. Label them so \(u=v_2(p-1)\ge v=v_2(q-1)\). For \(u>v\), choose the quadratic character \(\chi=(\cdot/p)\). Whenever \(\chi(\ell)=-1\), the 2-part of the order of \(\ell\bmod p\) is exactly \(2^u\) and that modulo \(q\) is at most \(2^v\). For \(u=v\), take \(\chi=(\cdot/p)(\cdot/q)\). Here \(\chi(\ell)=-1\) makes the 2-part maximal modulo exactly one of the two primes. This uses cyclicity of each prime-field unit group. In both cases the chosen character is nonprincipal, and no knowledge of either hidden prime enters the test. Raising \(\ell\) to the odd part of its verified exponent removes the odd parts of both local orders. During the squarings one of \(p,q\) consequently divides \(x-1\) while the other does not. This gcd is proper also when higher powers of either prime divide \(N\).

For \(B=2^{L^{.36}+O(1)}\) the proof of majority generation bounds the signed dyadic count by \(o(T)\) uniformly for these characters: first make the fixed taper loss arbitrarily small, then send \(N\) to infinity. Thus \((1/2+o(1))T\) target primes have negative sign, and only \(o(T)\) targets can fail to yield a verified exponent. At least \((1/2-o(1))T\) individual primes therefore qualify. A test costs \(O(\log E_\ell)\) multiplications or squarings modulo \(N\) and at most that many gcds. Success uses just one certified exponent for its own base. The replay above additionally tests all predicted negative-sign witnesses for 104 odd inputs with two or more distinct prime factors, including 42 with tied leading valuations. All 26,768 chains from exact and inflated orders return a proper divisor.

The Lean kernel checks that the gcd scan returns a proper divisor whenever one tested residue is one modulo a divisor and fails that congruence modulo another divisor. It also checks the concrete certificate for $N=77$, base $2$, exponent $30$, giving the divisor $7$. The character-density and relation-supply proofs are separate mathematical inputs.
