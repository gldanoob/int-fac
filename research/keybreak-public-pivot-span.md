# Public pivots for the one-field KeyBreak extractor

Classical scalar relations remove at most one parity dimension at a completely split prime. They remove none at the other good primes in the primitive linear-mark model. The actual randomized polynomial has only about one completely split prime per \(d!\) good factor-base primes. A different public construction gives isolated pivots for almost all rational and simple-root columns in the top factor-base dyad, at the cost of an additional terminal search sweep. This supplies rank at the factor-base exponent. A smaller residual exponent requires a stronger coverage or span estimate than the present concentration bound. The analysis uses the same single-input field and Dirichlet GRH assumptions as the [tiered schedule](keybreak-tiered-descent.md). [KeyBreak, Appendix E](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=85)

## Scalar relations and the residual columns

Write \(K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) for the odd-degree field, \(\gamma\) for the algebraic product in a row, and \(q\) for its rational correction. In the product of the rational and algebraic squareclass groups, quotient by all publicly known scalar relations \((a^{-1},a)\), where \(a\in\mathbb Q^\times\) is supported at checked units. On those classes the quotient map is
\[
 ([q],[\gamma])\longmapsto
 \bigl([q\,\operatorname{Nm}\gamma],[\gamma/\operatorname{Nm}\gamma]\bigr).
\]
The second component has trivial norm squareclass. The kernel consists of the scalar relations because the norm on rational squareclasses is the identity in odd degree. Every pair of a rational class and a norm-trivial field class lies in the image. The combined rational sign survives along with the combined prime parities. Quadratic characters applied to the norm-normalized class already vanish on scalar relations. Equivalently the square-root construction can use \(q/\delta\) square for the signed norm representative \(\delta\), with the separate field-square conditions still enforced. [KeyBreak, E.3–E.4 and the parity columns](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=90)

At a good unramified prime \(p\), let the prime ideals have residue degrees \(f_1,\ldots,f_s\), and let \(r_p\) have degree one. Denote the rational parity by \(u\) and the ideal parity vector by \(v\). The scalar anchor identifies \((1,\boldsymbol 1)\) with zero. An equivalent normalization is
\[
 n(v)=\sum_j f_jv_j,\qquad
 (u,v)\longmapsto \bigl(u+n(v),v+n(v)\boldsymbol 1\bigr).
\]
The kernel of this map is exactly the scalar diagonal since \(\sum_j f_j=d\) is odd. The ideal parity vector of any original primitive linear-mark row is supported on the degree-one ideals by Lemma E.1. Its ambient space together with the rational coordinate has dimension \(1+r_p\). The kernel meets that space only when \(p\) splits completely, in which case its intersection has dimension one. Thus the quotient has dimension \(1+r_p\) on the original ambient space at a prime with any higher-degree ideal, and dimension \(d\) at a completely split prime. Dropping a higher-degree coordinate introduced by a scalar relation would require an equivalent constraint. This computation characterizes the permitted column space and does not give a lower bound for the rank of acquired private rows. [KeyBreak, Lemma E.1](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=87)

In particular a rootless prime still has its rational coordinate. Keeping a positive-density subset of primes of size \(B=\exp((b+o(1))H)\) leaves the factor-base exponent \(b\). More generally, for any set of at most \(J\) rational primes and any positive Rankin exponent, its Euler product is at most that for the first \(J\) primes. If \(J=\exp((b'+o(1))H)\), \(b'>0\), their largest prime is \(\exp((b'+o(1))H)\). For values with logarithm \((s+o(1))DH\), the ordinary Rankin choice therefore gives an upper density of \(\exp(-(s/(3b')+o(1))H)\). A positive dyadic density lets elementary prime-product constructions retain the leading global smooth count. This fact does not transfer the short progression estimates to an arbitrary field-dependent support.

## Splitting in the sampled field

Put \(\mathcal J=\{p:B/2<p\le B,\ p\text{ prime}\}\) and \(J=|\mathcal J|\). Fix the original environment \((Q,r)\) and take \(p\nmid2NQ\). Under uniform coefficient residues in (G.4), \(F(X,1)\) is uniform on the affine space of degree-at-most-\(d\) polynomials with prescribed nonzero value \(NQ^{-d}\) at \(x=r/Q\bmod p\). Indeed multiplication by \(G(X,1)\) sends the \(d\)-dimensional space of degree-at-most-\((d-1)\) polynomials bijectively to the polynomials vanishing at that point. Evaluation at any \(j\le d\) distinct other points is uniform and independent by interpolation.

Consequently, applying the odd and even Bonferroni bounds for a fixed number of terms to the event of no roots gives terms \(\binom{p-1}{j}p^{-j}\). Uniformly in \(\mathcal J\), these tend to \(1/j!\). Letting the fixed number of terms increase after taking the asymptotic limit gives a rootless probability \(e^{-1}+o(1)\). The expected number of distinct affine roots is \((p-1)/p\), and its second moment is bounded by two. For a specified \(d\)-element set of roots avoiding \(r/Q\), there is exactly one polynomial with those roots and the prescribed value. Thus the completely split probability is exactly \(\binom{p-1}{d}/p^d=(1+o(1))/d!\) in the dyad. [KeyBreak, (G.4) and Lemma H.1](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=105)

For the actual coefficient law these statements can be combined across primes. For a single discrete Gaussian coefficient of width \(\sigma\), the weight of a residue class modulo \(m\) differs from the integral divided by \(m\) by \(O(1)\), uniformly in the class, by comparison with the integrals of its two monotone halves. Its law modulo \(m\) therefore differs from uniform by \(O(m/\sigma)\) in total variation. For the \(d\) independent coordinates and \(m=pq\le B^2\), the error is \(O(dB^2/\sigma)\), plus the finite law error from H.1. Here \(\log\sigma=\Theta(DH)\), so this error is smaller than any fixed negative power of \(B\) or \(d!\). Under the uniform reference the two prime reductions are independent. Chebyshev gives \((e^{-1}+o(1))J\) rootless primes and \((1+o(1))J\) distinct linear roots summed over the dyad with probability \(1-o(1)\). The root-count comparison may multiply the total variation loss by \(d^2\), which is still negligible. Good primes require removal of the polynomially many factors of \(2NQ\) and the field discriminant and leading coefficient. These removals affect the summed linear-root count by only a polynomial in \(\log N\). The same second-moment argument gives \((1+o(1))J/d!\) completely split good primes when \(b>\delta/3\), where \(d\sim\delta D\); the polynomial-sized deletion is negligible in that range. Over the full base the expectation is at most \(\pi(B)/d!+\exp(-\Omega(DH))\) before those deletions. In particular the number of potentially effective scalar pivots has exponent at most \(\max(0,b-\delta/3)\) with probability \(1-o(1)\). These are assertions on the original environment and coefficient law. They may be intersected with that law's existing good-parent event.

## Isolated pivots from one atom

Use a last active internal box of denominator scale \(V=\exp((v+o(1))H)\), with \(V/B\) exponentially large, the accepted primitive form from that original field, and \(w=-G(a,b')>0\). Let \(s_R,s_A\) bound the respective leading losses for the rational and algebraic smoothness predicates in that box. Use a terminal box with a fixed positive margin \(2v-b>s_R+s_A\), and retain the separate-period and finite Gaussian margins of its schedule. This is the same absolute mean check as at the last active level, with the size of a target prime in \(\mathcal J\) in place of the last-level parent upper bound. Set \(B'=\lfloor B/2\rfloor\).

| Target at \(p\in\mathcal J\) | Rational predicate | Algebraic predicate | Incoming lattice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rational parity at \(p\) | \(w/p\) is \(B'\)-smooth | \(|F(P)|\) is \(B'\)-smooth | \(p\mid G(P)\) |
| Ideal parity at a simple root \((p,s)\) | \(w\) is \(B'\)-smooth | \(|F(P)|/p\) is \(B'\)-smooth | \(a\equiv s b'\pmod p\) |

Use the source's prime denominator interval, exact rank sampler, unit tests, and factor-list verification. Zero values are rejected. For every verified mark the identity
\[
 1=\bigl(Q/G(P)\bigr)\,\psi(a-b'\alpha)\pmod N
\]
gives a row with known public square root \(1\). Its high-dyad projection onto rational and degree-one ideal columns is the specified unit vector. In the rational case the denominator and smooth cofactor are prime to all other dyad primes and the algebraic value is \(B'\)-smooth. In the ideal case \(p\) occurs to exponent one, the simple root identifies its exact ideal, and \(w\) carries no high-dyad divisor. The other parity columns, including sign and characters, are recorded as usual; their values have no effect on independence of the projected pivots. No private root or descent tree is used. [KeyBreak, Lemma E.1 and Appendix E.6](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=95)

For the *rational* target, the source input is the **individual rational-parent** argument of (G.18), with \(V\) in place of \(V_0\) and \(B'\) as the smoothness cutoff. This is not an ordinary algebraic-root request. Write \(w=pu\), so \(pu\equiv rb'\bmod Q\); the product convolution \(ub'\) has multiplicity one since \(b'\) is prime and \(b'\gg B'\). The denominator is also prime to \(p\) because \(b'\gg B\). Multiplication by \(p^{-1}\) modulo \(Q\) permutes residues; the whole and cell mean-square bound can sum over the full unit residue group before imposing the environment interval. Consequently the individual-target whole and cell count error is bounded by the (G.18) expression with the new scales,
\[
 K^4(\log N)^{O(1)}\rho_p^{-1}(p/V^2+1/M),
 \qquad \rho_p=\widehat\rho(MV/p,B')
\]
up to polynomial factors and fixed endpoint constants. The eligible count is comparable to \(\rho_pV^2/(p\log V)\) outside this failure event. The thin-pair bound follows from the same cell count. There is no aggregate distinguished-prime product convolution here and no coefficient-root conditioning. Use the individual-parent \(q=1\) ordinary polynomial comparison of G.6 and G.10 on this fixed eligible set. [KeyBreak, (G.18) and Proposition G.10](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=108)

For the *ideal* target, use the ordinary rational estimates (G.12)–(G.16) averaged over the root \(s\) in that box, and G.6 and G.10 conditioned on \(F(s,1)\equiv0\bmod p\). The excluded root \(r/Q\) cannot be a root because \(p\nmid NQ\). The proof needs root-average control, not a uniform rational count at each fixed root. For each \(s\ne r/Q\), the coefficient-root event has conditional probability \(O(1/p)\) for every admissible environment by the coefficient comparison above. Multiplying the sum of the rational failure probabilities by this factor, and using the conditional G.10 probability once the rational count holds, bounds the expected number of failed ideal labels over one \(p\). Simplicity and goodness are checked before recording the ideal pivot. [KeyBreak, Lemma G.3 and Proposition G.10](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=116)

Halving the smoothness cutoff and removing or inserting one factor \(p\) of size \(\exp(O(H))\) change each smoothness exponent by \(o(H)\). In particular the unconditioned \(q=1\) algebraic comparison for the rational target still has the stated terminal leading loss, though the smooth density need not change by only a constant factor. The supports, widths and separate periods in G.26–G.38 change in logarithm by at most \(O(H)\) in a late box, below their fixed \(\Theta(DH)\) margins. The ratio \(p/V^2\) times the inverse rational density is exponentially small by the mean hypothesis. For either target whose rational count holds, the absolute algebraic mean is at least
\[
 \exp\bigl((2v-b-s_R-s_A+o(1))H\bigr).
\]
This exceeds the lower threshold used for the diagonal remainder in the retuned G.10 proof when that threshold is chosen within the same strict mean margin. Applying only a normalized smoothness probability without this check would not justify concentration. The supply statement here relies on these local comparisons of the source and the same preserved margins as in the terminal schedule. It does not apply to a new box merely from its leading density expression. [KeyBreak, (G.25)–(G.38) and Proposition G.13](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=123)

Summing the rational-target failure probabilities from the individual estimate and the ideal-target failures using the root average gives \(o(J)\) expected failed targets, on the original environment and field law. Markov then gives \(o(J)\) failures with probability \(1-o(1)\). The G.10 term \(D^{-3A}\) is sufficient for this conclusion, and a preselected individual ideal label need not have that failure probability before averaging over roots. On each good target the verified point rate under the exact rank sampler is at least \(\exp(-(s_R+s_A+o(1))H)\). One can run \(\exp((s_R+s_A+\epsilon)H)\) bounded trials at each listed target, for any fixed \(\epsilon>0\) allowed by the work budget. The missed-search probability on a good target is then exponentially small in the trial ratio. Genus-two recognition at cutoff \(B'\) costs \(\exp(o(H))\) per trial and the confidence overhead can cover all calls at once. Bad targets are charged their full caps. Prime enumeration, bounded finite-field root finding, and the \(O(dJ)\) records take \(\exp((b+o(1))H)\) bit work. The whole search sweep is bounded by \(\exp((b+s_R+s_A+\epsilon+o(1))H)\). Indexing the recorded high-dyad pivots can use a sorted list. In a batch elimination, route all requests through that list and keep each reused occurrence with its multiplicity. Each private row has \(\exp(o(H))\) occurrences before this substitution, so adding one atom per routed high-dyad entry does not change that per-row exponent.

## Rank and the missing coverage bound

The acquired public rows have rank at least \((2-o(1))J\) in the dyad projection with probability \(1-o(1)\), because the dyad has \(J\) rational columns and \((1+o(1))J\) simple roots. The scalar quotient removes only \(o(J)\) of these directions by the splitting estimate. In the full extractor let \(U\) be the span of verified public rows and \(V_{\rm par}\) the space of allowed parity data. After quotienting by \(U\), any private selection mapping to zero can be completed with a public selection. The public rows have fixed known square roots and can be built before sampling private roots. With one fresh private label per private row, a nonempty private selection retains the same hidden-sign probability, and \(\dim(V_{\rm par}/U)+1\) private rows suffice by dimension.

A leading improvement would need a verified public span with \(\dim(V_{\rm par}/U)\le\exp((b-\epsilon+o(1))H)\) at lower total work, or a private-selection argument that requires fewer rows without establishing that dimension bound. One dyad leaves lower dyads of the original exponent. Even coverage across all primes above \(B^\theta\) for fixed \(\theta<1\) would need a stronger column-loss bound than \(o(J)\): a fraction \(D^{-3A}\) can still leave \(\exp((b+o(1))H)\) missed columns. The corresponding probability, rank, and wider-range cost estimates are not provided by the single-dyad argument.

At the [1.922 public-span target](keybreak-public-span-schedule.md), the per-target sweep here would have leading coefficient about \(2.1594\) before its own cap slack. The low raw coefficient in that replay charges only \(0.720\) for the number of private rows, whereas this sweep starts from the full \(b\simeq0.9608\) factor base. Satisfying that target would require a cheaper public construction as well as a stronger rank bound.
