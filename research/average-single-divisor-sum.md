# Deterministic polynomial average time from one divisor sum

There is an unconditional deterministic algorithm which completely factors every $n$ given $n$ and the single ordinary divisor sum $M=\sigma(n)$, with polynomial **average bit cost** on uniformly distributed integers of each bit length. For every fixed $A>0$ it also finishes within a polynomial in $\log Z$ on all but $O_A(Z/(\log Z)^A)$ integers $Z/2<n\le Z$. It permits arbitrarily many factors. The time to compute $\sigma(n)$ is outside this bound.

The same algorithm uses any supplied multiple of $p+1$ for every remaining prime of multiplicity one. On squarefree inputs any multiple of $\operatorname{lcm}_{p\mid n}(p+1)$ works. The set of good inputs is uniform over those choices; for general multiples the arithmetic costs also depend polynomially on $\log M$. Bach, Miller and Shallit gave a randomized polynomial-time algorithm for every input from one $\sigma(n)$. Earlier deterministic work includes polynomial algorithms for certain factor patterns and bounds under analytic hypotheses. [Bach–Miller–Shallit, Theorem 2](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~glmiller/Publications/Papers/BMS86.pdf) · [Morain–Renault–Smith](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08444) · [Worst-case bound under a uniform zero-free strip](single-divisor-sum.md)

Pomykała and Radziejewski obtained quantitative deterministic almost-all results from the Euler totient, using small compositeness and character witnesses. The argument here uses quadratic residue algebras to handle the different order supply furnished by one divisor sum. Publication priority for this average divisor-sum bound has not been established. [Pomykała–Radziejewski](https://nutmic2019.imj-prg.fr/confpapers/FactoringWitnesses.pdf)

### An algorithm which always terminates

Use $Z$ equal to the power of two specified by the input bit length. At stage $L$, remove all prime factors through $L$ with their multiplicities. Start at a fixed large power of $\log Z$ and double $L$ until the factorization is complete. The residual factor is tested by deterministic primality tests and by the following gcds. Each gcd refines the current list of divisors by splitting any entry on which it is proper. Accept only verified primes accounting for the entire input. If needed, the stage $L\ge\sqrt Z$ suffices by small-factor extraction alone.

Write $M=2^v D$ with $D$ odd. Use a split reference test consisting of the gcds with $a$ and with $a^{2^iD}-1$ for all small positive integers $a$ and $0\le i\le v$. Also use the quadratic orders $\mathbb Z[\sqrt d]$ for $d=2$ and every odd prime $d\le (\log Z)^{O(1)}$. For each nonzero small element $a+b\sqrt d$, test its integral norm by gcd and test the coefficient of $\sqrt d$ in each power $(a+b\sqrt d)^{2^iD}$. All coordinates can be taken within a fixed power of $\log Z$. For concrete polynomial cutoffs one may put $w=1+\lceil\log_2 Z\rceil$, start at $L=w^{10}$ or the final stage if smaller, and use all prime $d\le w^9$ and coordinates of absolute value at most $w^{2501}$. For these choices take $D_0=17$, $B=5000$ in the quadratic generation proof and exponent 32 for rational generators. Constants for finitely many small inputs can be absorbed by running the final stage. Each field sweep has polynomial bit cost.

Small-factor extraction through $L$ costs $O(\sqrt L\,\mathrm{poly}(\log Z))$. For $h=\lceil\sqrt L\rceil$, evaluate $\prod_{j=1}^h(X+j)$ at $0,h,\ldots,(h-1)h$ modulo the input using monic product and remainder trees. Check the gcds of these products with the current residual. Within a nonunit block inspect its at most $h$ entries in increasing order and strip each newly found prime to full multiplicity. Earlier entries have already removed all smaller prime factors. At most $\log_2 n$ blocks remove a distinct prime, with one extra block possible at the clipped endpoint. Values computed modulo the input remain valid modulo every residual divisor. [Pollard–Strassen method, Costa–Harvey, §1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.2116#page=1)

### Separating any two remaining primes

For squarefree residuals, $t+1\mid M$ at each residual prime $t$ by multiplicativity of $\sigma$. This also holds for primes of original multiplicity one when other primes had higher multiplicity. Define the threshold for a cyclic group of order $e$ as the first $0\le i\le v$ with $e\mid2^iD$, or infinity if none. At $t$, the split reference has order $t-1$. The quotient of the quadratic residue algebra units by scalar units is cyclic of order $t-(d/t)$ at $t\nmid2d$. In the split case this is the ratio of the two coordinates; in the inert case it is the finite-field quotient. The inert threshold is always $v_2(t+1)$. The split threshold is either $v_2(t-1)$ or infinity. These thresholds are distinct for every odd prime.

Suppose $p<q$ are good local primes in the sense below. If their split-reference thresholds differ, a small rational unit witnesses this via the power gcds. If they agree, it suffices to have one tested $d$ with $(d/p)\ne(d/q)$. On its split side the threshold stays equal to the common reference value; on its inert side it changes. At a level between the two thresholds all units on one side have scalar powers, while some small generator on the other side has a nonscalar power. If this generator is a nonunit on the first side, the norm gcd separates the pair instead. Thus individual gcds suffice even when a generator is a nonunit at some other prime divisor of $n$. If every pair is distinguished, successive refinements give the complete prime partition. On a squarefree input proper gcds refine disjoint prime supports.

### Uniform generation in the quadratic orders

Fix any $D_0>0$ and any $\epsilon>0$. Apart from $O(T^\epsilon)$ primes $t\in[T,2T]$, small integral coordinates, bounded by $(\log T)^{C(D_0,\epsilon)}$, generate the full residue unit group at $t$ in every field above with $d\le(\log T)^{D_0}$. The same bound holds for the rational reference. Here and below only the elements which are units at $t$ enter its local generation assertion.

To see this, use $Y=(\log T)^B$ with $B$ a sufficiently large fixed constant. Maynard's lower bound for primes $s\equiv1\bmod4d$ up to $Y$ gives $\gg Y/((4d)^{3/2}\log Y)$ split markers for $Y\ge(4d)^8$, also for bounded moduli by the prime number theorem in progressions. At each $s$ a root of $d\bmod s$ defines an evaluation lattice of index $s$. Pigeonholing a square of side $O(\sqrt s)$ gives a nonzero $a+b\sqrt d$ in it. Both conjugates are $O(\sqrt{ds})$ in absolute value and the absolute norm is $s$ times an integer $\ll d$. Discard markers $s\le C d$. The remaining markers distinguish the products of distinct elements through their norms, and all selected markers remain units at every $t\ge T$ for large $T$. [Maynard, Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.1777#page=3)

Products of $r=(4/(B+D_0)+o(1))\log T/\log\log T$ distinct markers fit in the coordinate box $[-T^2,T^2]^2$ by the conjugate bounds, rounding $r$ down as necessary. Their log count is at least $r((B-3D_0/2-1)\log\log T+O(1))$. In a proper subgroup of residue units they occupy at most half the $t^2$ ring elements. The two-dimensional additive large sieve therefore bounds the exceptional prime count by $O(T^4)$ divided by this product count. Its exponent tends to zero as $B$ increases with $D_0$ fixed. Increase $B$ to absorb the union over the polylogarithmically many $d$. Degree one gives the rational assertion. Summing $1/t$ over all exceptional primes $t>L$ now costs $O(L^{-1+\epsilon})$ after adjusting exponents. [Product count, additive large sieve and exact group checks](small-algebra-generators.md)

### Small quadratic sign witnesses

Fix $b=8$ in this paragraph. For comparable logarithmic sizes we use a one-dimensional sieve for the product modulus $w=pq$. In a dyadic interval $W\le w\le2W$, consider pairs whose prime factors exceed $(\log(2W))^b$. If the primitive character $\chi=(\cdot/p)(\cdot/q)$ is one on every prime up to $(\log W)^b$, it is one on all products of $r=(2/b+o(1))\log W/\log\log W$ distinct such primes, with $r$ chosen so the products are at most $W^2$. Their number is $|S|\ge W^{2(1-1/b)+o(1)}$. The normalized additive Fourier coefficients of $\chi$ have squared sum at most one and are supported only at fractions of **exact denominator $w$**. Indeed each prime Legendre character has mean zero and the expansion factors by CRT. Cauchy–Schwarz gives
$$
 |S|^2\le\sum_{(h,w)=1}\left|\sum_{a\in S}\exp(2\pi i ah/w)\right|^2 .
$$
Over distinct $w$ these frequencies are separated by $1/(4W^2)$. The additive large sieve bounds the number of such moduli by $W^{2/b+o(1)}$. One can omit the coprimality restriction on the input pairs if divisors found among the markers are handled separately. The above restricted version suffices.

At a stage $L\ge(\log Z)^{b+2}$ use in the proof $m=\lceil2\log_2 L\rceil$ distinct primes $d_i\equiv1\bmod4$ in increasing order and $Q=\prod_i d_i$. Then $d_m=O(\log L\log\log(3L))$, $\log Q=O(\log L\log\log(3L))$, and every $d_i\le(\log p)^2$ for residual $p>L$ sufficiently large. If $q\ge Q^2$, reciprocity and CRT give $\varphi(Q)2^{-m}$ reduced classes of $q$ having the same signs as a given $p$ at all $d_i$. Brun–Titchmarsh and partial summation bound their reciprocal sum up to $Z$ by $O(2^{-m}\log\log Z)$. Summing $1/p$ costs another $O(\log\log Z)$. Thus the fraction of inputs divisible by any such unresolved pair is $O(L^{-2}(\log\log Z)^2)$. [Montgomery–Vaughan, interval Brun–Titchmarsh bound](https://personal.science.psu.edu/rcv4/personal/Publications/large_sieve.pdf#page=3)

If $q<Q^2$, then $\log(pq)\ll\log L\log\log(3L)\ll(\log p)^2$. A sign witness up to $(\log(pq))^b$ is therefore within the uniform generation range at both primes, taking $D_0=2b+1$. Dyadic summation of the preceding exceptional modulus bound for $w>L^2$, weighted by $1/w$, costs $L^{-2+4/b+o(1)}$. These two ranges explain why a single library of fields of polylogarithmic size works even for highly unbalanced prime divisors. Only the proof selects $m$ or uses the hidden sizes of the primes.

### The average

The fraction of integers $n\in(Z/2,Z]$ with any repeated prime divisor greater than $L$ is $O(\sum_{j>L}j^{-2})=O(1/L)$. Every event above concerning divisibility by $t$ or $pq$ has probability at most $2/t$ or $2/(pq)$. Combining the bounds, the fraction of inputs still unresolved after stage $L$ is $O(L^{-1+\epsilon})$, say with $\epsilon=1/4$, uniformly from the initial cutoff to $\sqrt Z$. A stage costs $O((1+\sqrt L)\,\mathrm{poly}(\log Z))$, since $\sigma(n)\le n^2$. Its cost beyond the initial stage is incurred only on the unresolved fraction at the preceding stage $L/2$. The resulting dyadic series has terms $O(L^{-1/4}\,\mathrm{poly}(\log Z))$ and converges. This proves the deterministic average. At $L$ a sufficiently large power of $\log Z$, the same tail gives the stated arbitrary inverse-logarithmic exceptional fraction for a polynomial cutoff.


The finite replay uses much smaller cutoffs. From the one supplied divisor sum it completely factored $30513427075394312673352150800$, with small multiplicities $4,3,2,4$ and seven remaining primes from 1,009 to 1,039. Three quadratic scalar tests were needed after the rational tests. It also completely factored a seven-prime input from an enlarged $p+1$ multiple. A repeated prime outside the first extraction bound left a composite, and increasing the bound completed that input with the same supplied value. The block-extraction replay checks all multiplicities at a clipped endpoint. It uses direct polynomial evaluations for the small fixtures; the asymptotic bit cost uses multipoint remainder trees.
