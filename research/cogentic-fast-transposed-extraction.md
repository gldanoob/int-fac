# A subcubic tape implementation of the transposed extractor

Let $M$ be the number of retained sparse relation columns, $B$ the factor-base cutoff, and $L=\log N$. With $\omega_0=\log_2 7$, the transposed extractor admits an alternative bit-time ledger
$$
  \big(B^{\omega_0}+M B^{\omega_0-1}\big)\operatorname{poly}(L)
$$
and workspace $(B^2+M)\operatorname{poly}(L)$ on a multitape machine. This uses short-entry matrix multiplication, exact solving of a polynomial number of long right-hand sides, and the previously proved numerical guards. It applies once the sampling and analytic rank steps supply a relation list. [Precision bounds](cogentic-transposed-log-precision.md) · [Thinner-grid cost](cogentic-thin-denominator-candidate.md)

## Short-entry modular pivots on tape

With the original sampled prime $P_*\in[B^2,2B^2]$, keep a basis of original columns for the part of the relation list already read. Append the next block of at most $B\operatorname{poly}(L)$ columns and compute a CUP decomposition over $\mathbb F_{P_*}$. The first pivot columns after permutation form a basis of that block together with the old basis. The pivot rows form a row basis, so their intersection with those columns is an invertible minor. Keeping only its original columns preserves the span at each block boundary. The standard prime-avoidance estimate therefore gives the same rational-rank success probability as before. [CUP, Algorithm 6 and Propositions 3 and 6](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~astorjoh/ple.pdf#page=16)

Use Strassen multiplication on fixed-width blocks. A depth-first execution copies each of the seven child inputs from the parent and combines its output by sequential scans. The block routing and rewinds per node have cost quadratic in the matrix dimension times a polynomial in the entry bit length and the logarithm of the dimension. Each level on the active stack has at most a constant number of matrices. Thus the tape multiplication has exponent $\omega_0$ and quadratic storage up to those factors. For integer products, reduce in a power-of-two ring large enough for the signed output coefficients. For a prime field, use its modular operations throughout. The same matrix exponent on tapes is also available from the explicit integer matrix multiplication bound of Harvey and van der Hoeven. [Matrix multiplication in the multitape model](https://www.texmacs.org/joris/zmatmult/zmatmult.pdf)

CUP's two row-half calls have disjoint row ranges at each recursion level. Its submatrix copies and permutations can be done by sorting records with row and column labels and merging the relevant permutation lists. Across a recursion level this routing is quadratic up to logarithmic factors. The triangular solves use the block recursion in Algorithm 2. Tile the wide dimension in the rectangular products. A block reused in several tile products can be emitted in each required tile by a sequential scan and routed by tile labels. In the CUP Schur complement the shared inner dimension is at most the lower row count. If the right block is narrower than the inner dimension, pad it to that dimension and charge this product to the triangular solve's existing bound. Thus the materialized tile operands at a node are at most a constant multiple of the active matrix area. Block calls can be executed depth first with the parent data at the preceding tape stack mark. The block rank computations cost $(B^{\omega_0}+MB^{\omega_0-1})\operatorname{poly}(L)$, with quadratic dense workspace plus the sparse relation list. [CUP block arithmetic, Algorithms 2 and 6](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~astorjoh/ple.pdf#page=13)

## Exact solves with long right-hand sides

Storjohann's integer system theorem charges an $R$-by-$R$ coefficient matrix $C$ and an integer right-hand side $w$ using
$$
 q=\max\{\log(2+\|C\|_\infty),\ \log(2+\|w\|_\infty)/R\}.
$$
Up to logarithmic factors the cost is an $R$-dimensional matrix product with $O(q+\log R)$-bit entries. This is an integer bit bound that explicitly includes long right-hand sides. The quasi-linear integer gcd and reconstruction bounds satisfy the theorem's comparison with the Strassen multiplication cost divided by dimension. In our application $R\le B\operatorname{poly}(L)$, and the precision in the determinant-gap proof can be chosen with $O((R+1)\operatorname{poly}(L))$ bits by using the actual pivot dimension in the Hadamard bounds. Clearing the dyadic denominator of a log row leaves a right-hand side of this same bit length. The selected primal systems have short right-hand sides. Thus $q=\operatorname{poly}(L)$ in both cases. Run the solve separately on each of the $\operatorname{poly}(L)$ right-hand sides. [Storjohann, Theorem 37](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~astorjoh/shifted.pdf#page=25)

For the tape implementation, Algorithm 34 already has a usable base prime, since $P_*\nmid\det C$. Its base $X$ is a power of that prime with $\log X=\operatorname{poly}(L)$, chosen so that $O(R)$ digits exceed the reconstruction modulus. The initial inverse modulo $X$ is obtained by fast triangular inversion modulo $P_*$ and Newton doubling of the modular inverse to that *short* modulus. Subsequent calls to the short product subroutines do a short-entry matrix multiplication followed by entrywise digit operations and guard tests. In the long right-hand-side products, reshape the $O(R)$ digits of each entry into panels with one matrix column per digit. For one right-hand side the panels are $R$ by $O(R)$, and the short multiplier is $R$ by $R$. The products are again charged to a constant number of short-entry square products. Convert between binary and base $X$ via divide-and-conquer conversion, with sequential scans for carries and shifts and tape sorting for the digit rearrangements. The total digit array size is $R^2\operatorname{poly}(L)$. At most logarithmically many matrix stages occur in one solve. Each random shift fails with probability at most $1/2$, and the solve can reject after $(\log(2L))^2$ failed shifts to bound its cost on an unsuccessful trial. [Storjohann, Algorithms 32 and 34 and Theorem 33](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~astorjoh/shifted.pdf#page=22)

The binary/base-$X$ conversions and fast half-gcd reconstruction use a depth-first divide-and-conquer stack of integer operands with sequential copies. Quasi-linear integer multiplication and division, together with this routing, give a quasi-linear cost in the bit height per coordinate up to logarithmic factors. The same holds for accumulating a common denominator and rounding the rational log solutions back to dyadics. These stages use $B^2\operatorname{poly}(L)$ time or less. [Brent and Zimmermann, §§1.4, 1.6 and 1.7](https://members.loria.fr/PZimmermann/mca/mca-cup-0.5.5.pdf) · [Integer multiplication on tapes](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2021/193-2/p04)

## Stream the projected logs and rank test

Keep the previous dyadic guards. Scale each positive argument into $[2,4]$ and carry its power-of-two correction separately. Brent's arithmetic-geometric mean algorithm evaluates the resulting logarithms to $P=B\operatorname{poly}(L)$ precision in $P\operatorname{poly}(L)$ tape operations. Keep the existing root bisections, which cost only $B^2\operatorname{poly}(L)$ in total because the number of roots is polynomial in $L$. For a batch of $B$ free columns, sort sparse requests by pivot row, merge with the saved transposed log rows, then sort the resulting partial values by free-column index. This costs $B^2\operatorname{poly}(L)$ per batch and fits the quadratic workspace. The exact Gram determinant test has only $\operatorname{poly}(L)$ entries at this precision. Apply fraction-free elimination with quasi-linear multiplication and exact division, paying $B\operatorname{poly}(L)$ per free column. Using quadratic arithmetic for that test would lose the saving on the per-column work. The final real solve, bounded-denominator recovery, exact finite checks, and order verification together fit $B^2\operatorname{poly}(L)$ after retaining the selected sparse columns. The cost after modular pivots and exact lifts is therefore $(B^2+MB)\operatorname{poly}(L)$. [Brent, fast logarithm on tapes](https://cr.yp.to/bib/1976/brent-elementary.pdf) · [Certified determinant gap and final recovery](cogentic-transposed-log-precision.md)

With the thin-menu cap $M\le\exp((b+2\eta)H)$ this replaces the algebraic coefficient $3b+2\eta$ by $\omega_0 b+2\eta$. The thin-menu proof with $c>2b$ still has a raw mark exponent at least $b+4\sqrt{2b}/(3b)$ in the limit, whose minimum is $3(8/9)^{1/3}$. The separate full-grid averaging proof has a weaker geometric condition and can therefore use the algebraic saving in its optimization.
