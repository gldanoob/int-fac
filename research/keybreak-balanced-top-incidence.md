# One-prime incidence in a balanced dyadic shape

Use disjoint complete dyads \(I_h=(2^{e-h-1},2^{e-h}]\), \(0\le h\le J\), and allowed prime sets of sizes \(n_h\asymp 2^{e-h}/e\), uniformly. Suppose \(k\to\infty\), \(J\ge1\) is polynomial in \(k\), \(J=o(e)\), and the smallest \(n_h\) exceeds every fixed power of \(k\). Set \(S=\lfloor kJ/2\rfloor\). A balanced integer is a product of \(k\) distinct allowed primes whose dyadic indices sum to \(S\). Let \(A\) be the set of these integers and \(A_q\) the subset divisible by an allowed prime \(q\). Then, uniformly in \(q\),
\[
\frac{|A_q|}{|A|}\asymp\frac{ke}{(J+1)q}.
\]
In particular, when \(e\) and \(k\) are polynomial in the degree scale \(D\), this is a \(D^{O(1)}\)-comparison with \(1/q\). This statement counts actual integers uniformly.

## Conditioning an ordered product

Give index \(h\) weight \(a_h=2^h n_h\); all weights are comparable. Choose \(k\) independent indices with probabilities proportional to \(a_h e^{\lambda h}\), and within each index draw a uniform allowed prime independently. Conditional on index sum \(S\), every ordered prime tuple has the same probability: its unconditioned probability is proportional to \((2e^\lambda)^S\). Conditional also on distinctness, every product has precisely \(k!\) orders.

Choose the tilt \(\lambda\) to make the mean of one index \(S/k\). The tilt satisfies \(|\lambda|=O(1/J)\) for \(k\) sufficiently large. To see the bracket, compare the untilted weights to a uniform law with constant upper and lower factors. A sufficiently large fixed positive tilt in the variable \(h/J\) concentrates it above \(3/4\) with probability greater than \(3/4\); the opposite tilt concentrates it below \(1/4\) with the same probability. The mean is continuous and strictly increasing in the tilt. The tilted law therefore has all atoms comparable to \(1/(J+1)\), uniformly.

Here is the local estimate needed for conditioning. For any law on \(0,\ldots,J\) with these atom bounds, its variance is \(\Theta(J^2)\). Its characteristic function is bounded in modulus by \(1-c\) for \(c_0/J\le|\theta|\le\pi\), for each fixed sufficiently small \(c_0>0\) and a corresponding \(c>0\). Indeed the law contains a fixed uniform component, and the geometric-sum formula gives this gap for the uniform law. More explicitly, for \(C/J\le|\theta|\le\pi\) use \(|\sin(\theta/2)|\ge|\theta|/\pi\) with \(C\) large. For the remaining compact range of \(J|\theta|\), the normalized sinc limit gives the gap for large \(J\); finitely many small \(J\) have the gap because their support includes consecutive integers. In \(|\theta|\le c_0/J\), expansion of the centered characteristic function gives logarithm \(-\operatorname{Var}(h)\theta^2/2+O(J^3|\theta|^3)\), and modulus at most \(\exp(-c'J^2\theta^2)\). Fourier inversion and the substitution \(x=J\sqrt{k}\theta\) now give the Gaussian local limit uniformly for target sums at distance \(O(J)\) from their mean. On bounded \(x\)-intervals the expansion error is \(O(|x|^3/\sqrt{k})\), the rest of the small-angle integral is controlled by the Gaussian tail, and \(J\sqrt{k}(1-c)^k=o(1)\) controls the outer integral. The same estimate holds for \(k-1\) indices. Thus both the probability of total \(S\) and that of the remaining sum \(S-h\) after any first index are \(\Theta(1/(J\sqrt{k}))\).

By Bayes, the first index given \(S\) has each atom \(\Theta(1/(J+1))\). Its probability of drawing \(q\in I_h\) is that atom divided by \(n_h\). At any fixed index tuple, even with one designated prime fixed, the probability of any repeated prime is \(O(k^2/\min n_h)=o(1)\), by the union bound. Conditioning on distinctness therefore changes the first-slot incidence by a factor \(1+o(1)\) uniformly. In the distinct model, the \(k\) events that a slot contains \(q\) are disjoint and equiprobable, giving the claim. It also shows directly that every allowed \(q\) has completions for sufficiently large parameters.

## What this yields for a reference density

If \(X\) is a reference interval length comparable to \(\lvert I\rvert\) and \(I\) contains the entire balanced product strip \([2^{ke-S-k},2^{ke-S}]\), use \(|A|/X\) as the reference density. For the divided variable in the \(q\)-restricted test, use \(|A_q|/(X/q)\). The two densities then have ratio \(\Theta(ke/(J+1))\), polynomial in \(D\) for the stated scales. The same comparisons tolerate a common profile weight lying between polynomially comparable positive constants throughout the strip. If the ordinary saddle-point parameter satisfies \(\gamma\log y=O(\log D)\) and \(q\le y\), replacing \(1/q\) by \(q^{\gamma-1}\) also changes the bounds by only polynomial factors.

Independent proposals can choose the tilted indices and reject unless their sum is \(S\); this costs \(O(J\sqrt k)\) proposals in expectation. Fixing one slot to \(q\) uses the same bound for the remaining sum; repeated dyads cause no additional material rejection. Sampling within an allowed prime set is a separate operation, and the argument gives no procedure for recognizing field-good primes.

These comparisons use the whole strip or a positive comparable profile throughout it. For an arbitrary interval or progression, the strip count is insufficient: a region in a nonzero residue class modulo \(q\), for example, can have unforced balanced values and zero \(q\)-forced values. Even an upper incidence comparison against the ordinary local density would need a lower bound on balanced completions in the retained region. Consequently the balanced-reference comparison by itself supplies no replacement for the smooth-value progression estimates in a top moment proof.
