# Factoring integers with a fixed hidden common divisor

Fix an integer \(r\ge1\). There is a single deterministic algorithm that receives \(N\), emits certified primes with their full multiplicities in \(N\), and eventually returns its complete factorization. Its performance has two uniform guarantees. Write
\[
 \mathcal L(x)=\exp\!\left(C_r\sqrt{\log(2x)\log(2+\log(2x))}\right)
\]
for a sufficiently large constant depending only on \(r\).

* Let \(K\ge2\) be fixed, \(P\) its largest prime divisor, and \(m\) uniform on any interval of \(H\ge1\) consecutive positive integers \(A<m\le A+H\). Set \(b=\lceil\log_2(2+K(A+H))\rceil\). If \(T_K(Km)\) is the time when the algorithm has emitted every distinct prime of \(K\), then
  \[
    \big(\mathbb E_m T_K(Km)^r\big)^{1/r}
     \le b^{O_r(1)}\min\{\sqrt P,\ (1+P^{3r}/H)^{1/r}\mathcal L(P)\}.
  \]
  The interval can have an arbitrarily large offset. The definition of \(T_K\) is an observable stopping time for an analyst who knows \(K\). The algorithm emits each prime as soon as it certifies it and needs only \(N\).
* For \(Z<m\le2Z\) with \(Z\ge1\) integer and \(P^3\le Z\), the **arithmetic mean time to complete the factorization of \(Km\)** is at most \(b^{O(1)}\mathcal L(Z)\), using the algorithm at \(r=1\). This includes all prime powers in both \(K\) and \(m\), primes shared by them, and the rest of the partner after recovering the primes of \(K\).

The inputs include the proved ordinary Riemann hypothesis for the short smooth-number estimates and the proved Dirichlet GRH for the uniform sign witnesses. Lenstra–Pila–Pomerance developed the probabilistic genus-two search. Lenstra–Pomerance gave rigorous expected complete factoring on each input by random class-group operations. Here the randomness in the stated averages comes solely from the choice of the partner integer. ## A search that preserves witnesses through successive splits

For each prime-size dyad \(D<p\le2D\) and binary shift \(j\), run a copy of the [three-coordinate genus-two search](three-parameter-genus-two.md). Enumerate powers of two \(D\) up to the input and \(0\le j\le\operatorname{bits}(N)\). These polynomially many copies can each keep their own list of unresolved factors, initially containing \(N\). Dovetail individual bit steps, including trial division, scalar preparation, and the work at unaffordable guesses. Each copy gets a polynomial fraction of execution time. Whenever any copy certifies a prime \(p\), emit \((p,v_p(N))\) on its first appearance by repeated division of the original input. Primality tests and integer-root tests recognize a prime or a pure prime power; all reported splits are checked by division. Duplicated primes and overlapping noncoprime factors are allowed inside separate searches.

Each copy computes its binary starts from the **original** \(N\) throughout. At \(2^k=2D\), take the low \(r\) consecutive blocks of \(3k\) bits from \(\lfloor N/2^j\rfloor\), adding leading zeros as needed. At stage \(a\) run all \(4^a\) small-bias shifts independently on each start, all the public sign candidates \(f\), and the deterministic conic marks and slopes. For fixed \(D\), the smooth cutoff, the mark prefix, and the scalar bound are kept when a block splits. In particular the odd scalar can use prime powers up to \(4N^2\). A copy first tries all small prime factors through its required cutoff; for the true dyad its bit cost is \(b^{O(1)}\mathcal L(p)\) on enlarging \(C_r\). The polynomial-height sign list uses the original input length. [Parameter cost and sign theorem](three-parameter-genus-two.md#conic-marks-independent-of-the-cubic) · [Least prime in a character coset, Theorem 1.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.3595#page=3)

For each parameter choice and \(f\), process every current unresolved block \(d\). Try its mark and slope menu modulo \(d\). A singular choice affecting all of \(d\) skips just that model. After any proper gcd split \(d=uv\), process both children with the **same parameter choice and \(f\)**, beginning the menu again. Test every child for primality and prime-power structure. This terminates, since proper splits in an entire copy form a multiplicative tree with at most \(\log_2 N\) final leaves and at most that many internal nodes. Thus the work per parameter grows by only a polynomial in \(b\), even when a single parameter causes several successive splits.

For each \(p\mid K\) above the small-prime cutoff and each public \(f\) with \(\chi_p(f)=+1\), the earlier construction supplies a target set of three-coordinate words of density \(\gamma_p\ge 1/\mathcal L(p)\), after increasing the constant. The set depends only on \(p,f\), its true dyad and the fixed menu. Every such word includes some marked class of smooth odd order at \(p\), while the same choice forces even order or a unit-test discrepancy at **every** other prime \(q\) with \(\chi_q(f)=-1\). For every pair of distinct odd primes \(p,q\) still present, Dirichlet GRH supplies an \(f\) in the menu with these signs. Small primes removed at the start of the correct copy cause no obstruction. [Target size and local curve discrepancy](three-parameter-genus-two.md#target-size-and-the-cost-of-selection)

As soon as the correct copy has hit the target of every eligible \(f\) for this \(p\), it has emitted \(p\). To prove this, suppose a pending block still contains \(p\) and some \(q\ne p\). Take their sign witness \(f\). Their common ancestor at the moment the target was hit contained both. On any block containing both, the good mark either triggers a proper gcd during a unit check or gives different reduced identity results in Cantor multiplication, which also triggers a proper gcd. A split that puts both primes in one child sends the same parameter back to that child. When this choice finishes processing its descendant blocks, no block contains that pair. Later refinement cannot reunite them. Once every partner prime is separated, a block containing only \(p\) is recognized as a pure prime power, or a previous block already emitted it. This argument permits mixed prime powers without any squarefreeness assumption.

## The common shift on any integer interval

For the analysis take the one shift \(j=\lfloor\log_2 K\rfloor\) for every \(p\mid K\). Then \(1\le K/2^j<2\), so \(m\mapsto\lfloor Km/2^j\rfloor\) is injective. Its image on the partner interval lies in an integer interval of length at most \(2H+1\), at any offset. Each residue modulo \(2^{3kr}\) is consequently attained at most \(O(1+H/2^{3kr})\) times. A target of cube density \(\gamma_p\) has at most a fraction \(\min(1,(3k)^2/(4^a\gamma_p))\) of missed single starts. For all \(r\) blocks to miss, take the Cartesian product of those missed sets. No independence between digits of the actual partners is required. [Small-bias inequality and full coverage](mean-time-elliptic-factoring.md#a-deterministic-search-with-a-summable-cost-per-stage)

Take the union over the polynomially many \(f\) and the at most \(b\) actual input bit lengths in the interval. For each eligible \(p\), the number of partners still missing some target at stage \(a\) of its correct copy is at most
\[
        (H+p^{3r})\,b^{O_r(1)}\mathcal L(p)^r4^{-ar}.
\]
The stage has cumulative cost at most \(4^a b^{O_r(1)}\mathcal L(p)\), with fixed constant powers of \(\mathcal L(p)\) absorbed into \(C_r\). Through \(a=3k\) there are \(O(b)\) stages, and the final shifts cover all three-coordinate words. Summing the \(r\)-th power of the next stage cost against the preceding miss bound, and including the first stage, gives the claimed moment for the time to emit this one \(p\), with \(p\) in place of \(P\). The time to emit all \(p\mid K\) is their maximum. Its \(L^r\) norm is bounded by the sum of their \(L^r\) norms, and there are at most \(b\) such primes.

In parallel, double an ordinary factorial search bound. At bound \(B\), a Pollard–Strassen block search on a composite with least factor at most \(B\) finds a proper divisor in \(\sqrt B\,b^{O(1)}\) bit operations, with direct checks inside a nonunit block. Apply it recursively to unresolved blocks until each is a recognized prime power or has no prime divisor at most \(B\). There are at most \(O(b)\) visits per level of the doubling schedule, as every successful split adds a leaf. A failure on a leaf is recorded for that level. Taking the first level \(B\ge P\) has therefore emitted every prime of \(K\) deterministically within \(\sqrt P\,b^{O(1)}\). This supplies the minimum in the first bound. [Factorial block search and its bit complexity](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1201.2116)

## Completing the residual factorization

Use the [deterministic complete algorithm with an ordinary arithmetic mean bound](mean-time-elliptic-factoring.md) as a background computation \(G(d)\) of actual cost \(t(d)\), with \(t(1)=O(1)\). Start one job on \(N\) and another after each new certified prime, on the residual after dividing **all** certified primes to full multiplicity. Store its list of already certified prime powers with each job. Its result therefore completes the original factorization, even if later primes have also been emitted. Dovetail the at most \(b+1\) jobs and the extraction searches with polynomial scheduling overhead. A completed background job also emits all of its certified primes. Thus completing the factorization can only advance the extraction time.

At time \(T_K(Km)\) the whole factorization is already known or a background job has begun on some \(d\mid m\). Its remaining cost is bounded by \(b^{O(1)}\max_{d\mid m}t(d)\), even if the last emitted prime also divides \(m\). For the dyad \(Z<m\le2Z\),
\[
 \frac1Z\sum_{Z<m\le2Z}\max_{d\mid m}t(d)
 \le \frac1Z\sum_{d\le2Z}t(d)\left\lfloor\frac{2Z}{d}\right\rfloor
 \le 2\sum_{d\le2Z}\frac{t(d)}d
 \le (\log(2Z))^{O(1)}\mathcal L(Z).
\]
For the last step use the existing mean bound on each dyadic block of \(d\) and sum over the \(O(\log(2Z))\) blocks. At \(r=1\) and \(P^3\le Z\), the extraction term has the same bound up to a polynomial in \(b\), since \(\mathcal L(P)\le\mathcal L(Z)\). The implementation receives no information about \(K,P,H\) or the interval. This mean estimate measures the partner size even when high prime powers or many distinct primes make \(K\) much larger than \(Z\). ## Finite replay

Run `gp -fq experiments/common-divisor-partners.gp`. The search receives only the product and uses a shortened parameter and mark menu, gcd-checked Cantor multiplication, prime-power tests, full multiplicities and new residuals after each certification. The five hidden common divisors range over products and repeated powers of two or three distinct primes. All 90 products were factored. There were 181 unit-test splits, 167 Cantor splits and 258 proper splits on children replaying their parent's word. The replay recognized 47 nontrivial pure prime powers. For 687 child calls, recomputing the start from the child would have given a different word. Among the fixtures, 26 share a prime between \(K\) and \(m\) and ten have a nontrivial residual at the first moment all primes of \(K\) have appeared. A direct finite trial-factor job reconstructs the original factorization independently from every residual. The shifted integer-word count was checked on 468 intervals, including offsets above \(2^{89}\), and the residual-divisor cost inequality on 99 dyads. These checks exercise the extraction and charging steps at reduced scale.
