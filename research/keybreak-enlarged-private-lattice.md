# Enlarged private representative boxes

Let $H=(\log N)^{1/3}(\log\log N)^{2/3}$, $D=H/\log\log N$, and enlarge the private box to $X=\sqrt N\exp(tH)$ for fixed $t\ge0$. Each unit private root $h$ exposes $R=h^2\bmod N$ and permits the public enumeration of positive primitive pairs $(u,v)$ with $u\equiv Rv\pmod N$ and $u,v\le X$. Keep both coordinates units. This changes the supply per square and loses the original uniqueness of a normalized representative. Complete enumeration followed by the same staged recognizers has no leading improvement even with the most favorable occupancy permitted by the first moment. Batch gcds of the enumerated coordinates almost never recover a permitted prime from a good pair. These bounds leave selective enumeration and a new rank argument for public rows open. [KeyBreak, §§H.5 and H.8](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=135) · [Staged private tests](keybreak-private-band-stages.md)

## Occupancy and one hidden root per square

Two distinct positive primitive pairs in one lattice have determinant a nonzero multiple $kN$ with $|k|\le 2X^2/N$. The sine of the angle between them is at least $N/(2X^2)$, so their directions in the positive quadrant have spacing at least $N/(2X^2)$. Thus the number per square is $O(1+X^2/N)$. A reduced lattice basis gives enumeration with that same bound up to polynomial factors in $\log N$. If the shortest vector has length $\lambda$, the component of the second basis vector perpendicular to it has length $N/\lambda$. The nonzero second coordinate in this basis has $O(X\lambda/N)$ possible integer values and each admits $O(1+X/\lambda)$ first coordinates. Gauss reduction gives $\lambda=O(\sqrt N)$. The shortest-vector line itself contributes at most one normalized positive primitive point. Unit and primitivity checks are exact, and a proper gcd with $N$ is an immediate factor. [KeyBreak, §H.5](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=135)

For $t>0$, the full primitive population is $\Theta(X^2/N)$ with probability $1-o(1)$ conditional on a unit draw for the rough inputs left by the prepass. For any $L<N$, group nonzero vectors with both coordinates at most $L$ in absolute value by $g=\gcd(v,N)$. Their coordinates are multiples of $g$, and each pair has at most $g$ possible residues $R$. The total raw block probability of finding such a vector is at most $O(2^{\omega(N)}L^2/N)\sum_{g\mid N}g^{-1}$. For $L=\sqrt N\exp(-\epsilon H)$ and fixed $0<\epsilon<t$, this tends to zero. At $L=X$, restricting the sum to $g>1$ also gives $o(1)$ since the logarithm of the smallest prime of $N$ grows faster than $H$ and the divisor count is $\exp(o(H))$. Thus a reduced basis has lengths $O(\sqrt N\exp(\epsilon H))$ and the nonzero vectors under consideration are units. A translated rectangle of basis coefficients inside the positive box then has both sides exponentially long in $H$, with product $\Theta(X^2/N)$. The coordinates of its center in that basis are bounded by constant multiples of the corresponding side lengths, since the reduced basis angle has sine bounded below. Counting pairs of nonzero coefficients both divisible by a prime removes at most a fraction $\sum_{k\ge2}k^{-2}+o(1)<1$, by summing the two interval-count errors through a constant times the shorter side length. Coprime basis coefficients give primitive integer vectors for unit pairs, since any common integer divisor coprime to $N$ can be divided out inside the lattice. This establishes raw occupancy.

Use the same fixed bands and length fractions as in the staged count, allocating the total logarithmic length up to $\log X$. Since $tH=o(\log N)$, this leaves each leading loss $w_i$ unchanged. Put $S_j=\sum_{i<j}w_i$ and $S=\sum_iw_i$. The H.20 product count gives a set of good coordinate products of size $X\exp(-(S/2+o(1))H)$. The original quadratic-class and common-prime counts still give $\exp((2t-S+o(1))H)$ good pairs per random private block **in expectation**, using the matching prefix Rankin upper bound for the other direction. In the large-prime case of the prepass, $2^{\omega(N)}=\exp(o(H))$, and the square-class count and the number of preimages of a unit square cancel in the lower bound. The angle bound then gives a hit probability at least $\exp(-(S+o(1))H)$. A gain in hit probability from spreading the candidate mass across squares would need control of collisions. The congruence $u_1v_2\equiv u_2v_1\pmod N$ admits nonzero determinants at this $X$. The original injection for $X<\sqrt N$ no longer applies. [Shape count and the rough-input prepass](keybreak-private-shape-work.md) · [KeyBreak, (H.18)–(H.20)](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=135)

Even with ideal occupancy, the hit probability is at most $\min(1,\exp((2t-S+o(1))H))$. If at most one row is retained per independently drawn root, obtaining $e^{(b+o(1))H}$ rows with constant probability therefore requires at least $e^{(b+(S-2t)_++o(1))H}$ private draws. The existing prefix density upper bound gives $\exp((2t-S_j+o(1))H)$ stage-$j$ pairs per draw in expectation. At the level of this first moment the exponent is attained by taking the counted prefix products and padding each with primes outside the permitted bands until it lies in the enlarged box. For any proper prefix, its remaining length is a positive fraction of $\log N$. For a prefix product $m$, two primes of size comparable to $\sqrt{X/m}$ give $X/(m\operatorname{poly}(\log N))$ paddings by the prime number theorem. The prime decomposition identifies $m$, and the square-class count and negligible shared-prime bound apply again. This counts entrants to an exhaustive stage, before any new filter at that stage.

Charging the same cost $e^{(h_*z_j+o(1))H}$ per stage-$j$ invocation gives the optimistic complete-sweep coefficient

\[
 b+(S-2t)_++2t-S_j+h_*z_j
 = b+\max\{S,2t\}-S_j+h_*z_j.
\]

For $2t\le S$ it equals the original stage coefficient. For $2t>S$ it rises. This comparison concerns explicit enumeration and invoking the same capped tests on every surviving candidate. It does not bound a search that skips candidate positions according to additional verified information.

A public choice of one representative per independently sampled square preserves the root symmetry of E.5. If multiple rows use the same private random block, each occurrence uses the same $h$. Let $A$ be the map from selected rows to parity columns and $B_0$ the map from rows to their root groups over $\mathbf F_2$. An acceptable dependency needs $Ax=0$ and $B_0x\ne0$. Such an $x$ is guaranteed if the number of distinct groups exceeds the number of parity columns, since $\operatorname{rank}B_0>\operatorname{rank}A$. Many rows in the same groups alone give no such rank guarantee. Differences of two same-group rows can give usable public relations, whose supply and rank require a separate bound. [KeyBreak, Theorem E.5 and §H.8](https://crypto.sjtu.edu.cn/factoring.pdf#page=93)

## Shared factors and sieve cutoffs

All permitted private primes exceed $Y_{\min}=\exp(\Theta(DH))$. Such a prime $q$ dividing one coordinate is a unit modulo $N$ and exceeds $2X^2/N$ for fixed $t$. If it divides both numerators or both denominators in a square, then $q\mid kN$ for the determinant multiplier $k$, which is impossible for distinct primitive pairs. This also rules out duplicate values in the same coordinate role whenever the value has a permitted prime.

For opposite roles, suppose $q\mid u_1$ and $q\mid v_2$ and at least one of the distinct pairs is good. After fixing $q,u_1,v_2,k$, the relation $u_2v_1=u_1v_2-kN$ fixes the product. One of $u_2,v_1$ comes from a good pair and has all its primes above $Y_{\min}$. Any product at most $X^2$ has at most $O(D)$ prime factors above this floor counted with multiplicity. Hence there are at most $2^{O(D)}$ choices for that coordinate, even if the other coordinate is unrestricted. Summing the resulting tuple bound using $\sum_{q\ge Y_{\min}}(X/q)^2=O(X^2/Y_{\min})$ and $O(X^2/N)$ choices of $k$ gives an upper bound per private draw of

\[
 O\left(\frac{2^{\omega(N)+O(D)}X^4}{N^2Y_{\min}}\right)
 = \frac{\exp((4t+o(1))H)}{Y_{\min}}.
\]

Here each tuple determines one $R$ and contributes at most $2^{\omega(N)}/2^n$ of the raw $n$-bit blocks. The other opposite-role orientation has the same bound. For independently sampled roots, fix any permitted prime seen in earlier coordinates. The chance that it divides a coordinate of any pair for a fresh draw is $O(2^{\omega(N)}X^2/(qN))$, by counting raw pairs with a coordinate divisible by $q$. There are only $\exp(O(H))$ raw coordinate records across an epoch of $\exp(O(H))$ draws, with at most $O(D)$ permitted primes per record. A union bound covers all cross-draw repeats. Thus with probability $1-o(1)$ no distinct enumerated coordinate shares a permitted prime of a good pair, even if the other pair fails recognition. Products of unrecognized records cannot supply such a prime by gcd. The same-record case just reproduces the coordinate.

A factor-base product with prime powers through $z$ has $O(z\log X)$ bits. A product/remainder tree can obtain the part of every candidate supported on this base with work quasi-linear in the aggregate operand and base lengths. At any affordable cutoff $z=\exp(O(H))$, this extracts none of the primes in a good coordinate. An explicit base extending to $Y_{\min}$ has size $\exp(\Theta(DH))$. A sieve may reject candidates with smaller factors. It also leaves the same leading population of the padded prefix examples above, since every prime in those examples exceeds the sieve cutoff. Likewise, packing separate curve calls into one product modulus shares the scalar and curve setup and still charges arithmetic on the sum of the operand lengths for each trial. These comparisons leave open a structured procedure that finds permitted factors or tests selected lattice positions without materializing the whole candidate set.
