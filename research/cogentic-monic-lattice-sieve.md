# Exact complete-parity collection with root lattices

The lattice collector returns the same ordered primitive marks, small and large ideal rows, and smooth flags as the complete-parity trial collector at cutoff $Y=2A^2$. It replaces the scan over all small primes at each mark with one search for the roots of each field polynomial modulo each prime, followed by exact division at lattice hits. At height 256 the seven existing holdouts took about 9 seconds in the lattice collector and 27 seconds under trial division. ## Root and mark enumeration

For a monic $P$ and a primitive mark $(a,b)$, if $p$ divides the homogeneous norm, then $p\nmid b$. Otherwise monicity would make the norm congruent to $a^{\deg P}$ modulo $p$. Thus the norm is divisible by $p$ exactly when the mark belongs to one of the lattices $a-rb\equiv0\pmod p$, where $r$ is a root of $P$ modulo $p$. The algorithm enumerates exactly the roots using the squarefree polynomial $R=\gcd(P,X^p-X)$ over $\mathbf F_p$. To split $R$ at odd $p$, try shifts $t$ in order and take $\gcd(R,(X+t)^{(p-1)/2}-1)$ until it is proper and nonconstant. Two distinct root values have different membership in the shifted nonzero-square set for some $t$. Equal membership for all $t$ would make that set invariant under a nonzero translation and hence under the entire additive group. Recursion therefore terminates, including when $P$ has multiple roots, and at two we test both residues directly.

For $p\le A$, loop over denominators and visit the congruent numerators in strides of $p$. For $p>A$, Gauss-reduce the basis $(p,0),(r,1)$, orient it so the determinant is $p$, and let $u=(x,y)$ with $y>0$ denote the first vector. If $r=0$, the only possible primitive mark is $(0,1)$. Otherwise both coordinates of $u$ are nonzero since a vector on either axis would have length at least $p$, greater than the length of $(r,1)$. Write a point as $j u+k v$. The box bounds $kp=xb-ya$. For every such $k$, intersect the two integer intervals on $j$ defined by $|a|\le A$ and $1\le b\le A$. On the line $k=0$ only $u$ can be primitive with positive denominator. Dense slots indexed by $(a+A)A+b-1$ discard hits outside the prefiltered primitive, unit grid without a membership search.

When a lattice hits a live mark, divide out the entire prime power and record the row $(i,p,r)$ if the valuation is odd. All other lattice primes leave this norm's residue intact. The allowable unramified and specified ramified rows, the optional mark-local derivative checks, and the integer square test on the tail are the same as in trial collection. With mark-local admission, a multiple residue root is allowed at valuation one. A multiple root at larger valuation rejects a mark, including when a prime above the cutoff divides the square tail and the homogeneous derivative. A nonsquare tail has a prime above the cutoff with odd valuation. Such a row is private to this mark since two distinct marks have nonzero determinant at most $2A^2$ in absolute value. The private row forces this mark's coefficient to zero in every joint dependency. ## Deterministic worst-case cost

Write $d$ for the greatest degree and $L$ for the maximum bit length of a norm, derivative value, input integer, discriminant product, or coefficient. The number of fields is fixed. Sieving the prime list takes $\widetilde O(Y)$ bit work. With deterministic polynomial gcd and modular powering, one residue-root computation per prime uses at most $O(1+dp)$ polynomial powers and gcds. Each successful split adds one piece, and at most $p$ shifts precede each split. The full root stage therefore costs $O(Y^2\operatorname{poly}(d,L,\log Y))$ bit operations under schoolbook arithmetic.

For $p\le A$, each residue root costs $O(A+A^2/p)$ visits and denominator steps. For $p>A$, Gauss reduction takes logarithmically many steps in $p$. If $\lambda=\|u\|_2$, then the reduced basis has $\|v\|_2\ge\lambda$ and $|u\cdot v|\le\lambda^2/2$, so its area satisfies $p\ge(\sqrt3/2)\lambda^2$. The number of nonzero $k$ is $O(A\lambda/p)$, and each permits $O(1+A/\lambda)$ positions for $j$. Including the line through zero, this gives $O(1+A/\sqrt p+A^2/p)$ work per residue root, apart from logarithmic reduction and arithmetic costs. Summing even over all integers through $Y=2A^2$ gives $\widetilde O(dA^2)$ lattice and slot operations. Prime divisions and parity entries contribute at most $O(A^2L)$ operations and entries, because every exact division decreases a positive residual by at least a factor of two. The norm, gcd, square-root and derivative work per mark is polynomial in $d$ and $L$. The slot arrays, row keys and stored prime roots use $O(A^2\operatorname{poly}(d,L,\log Y))$ bits. Thus the implemented collector costs at most $O(A^4\operatorname{poly}(d,L,\log A))$ bit operations overall, with the shift scan controlling the bound. This gives the same conservative power of $A$ as testing every prime at every mark. The measured speedup comes from doing the prime tests once per field and prime and only dividing at lattice hits. With $W=(\log N)^{1/3}(\log\log N)^{2/3}$, the schedule $A=\exp(O(W))$ and $d,L=\operatorname{poly}(\log N)$ preserves the $\exp(O(W))$ parity-stage bound.

## Exact comparisons and throughput

Run `python experiments/cogentic-monic-lattice-sieve.py` for the default height 64 and add `--height 256` for the larger grid. The replay asserts equality of the full ordered entries and their packed row words. It also checks the lattice against exhaustive small boxes, all four combinations of dyadic and local admission, and deliberately constructed simple square, multiple-root square and odd large-prime tails. The fixtures include a repeated bad prime both below and above the cutoff, and a multiple root at valuation one. These tests use the same known discriminant and grid parameters for both collectors.

The single-process timings include prime generation in both paths and the GP root search in the lattice path. Discriminant computation and equality checks are outside both timers. At height 64 and cutoff 8,192, the fixed holdouts took 0.323 seconds in trial collection and 0.720 seconds in lattice collection. At height 256 and cutoff 131,072, they took 26.690 and 9.087 seconds respectively, a 2.94-fold speedup. The height-256 replay reported a peak resident set of about 68 MiB.
